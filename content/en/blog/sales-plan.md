In many companies the sales plan is a single number: "300 million this month". Where it came from, how many leads it takes and who is supposed to sell what is anyone's guess. A proper **sales plan** is worked out backwards: from revenue to the number of deals, from deals to leads, and from leads to the ad budget. This article walks through that calculation, a plan for each rep, weekly tracking and motivation.

## Why "sell more" isn't a plan

A plan answers three questions: how much, who, and from where. "Sell more" or "20% up on last month" is a wish. A real plan includes:

- **a number** — monthly revenue and the number of sales;
- **a source** — where those sales come from: new leads, repeat customers, referrals;
- **an owner** — a personal target for each rep;
- **checkpoints** — interim weekly numbers, so you're not waiting for month-end.

Without a plan, problems surface at the end of the month, when it's too late to fix them. With one, the first week already tells you whether "we're short of leads" or "the leads are there but aren't converting".

## Step 1: pull the last 3 months of numbers

A plan starts from facts. Take five figures for the past three months from your CRM or spreadsheet:

| Metric | Where to find it |
|---|---|
| Number of leads | CRM, ad account, Telegram and phone |
| Qualified leads (meeting, measurement visit, consultation) | CRM stages |
| Number of sales | CRM or till |
| Average order value | Revenue ÷ number of sales |
| Share of revenue from repeat customers | Till or CRM customer base |

If leads live in reps' phones rather than in a CRM, fix that first — otherwise the plan is built on guesses. Our article on [choosing a CRM](/en/blog/crm-for-small-business/) covers which system fits.

## Step 2: from revenue target to number of leads

The core formula is simple: **revenue = leads × conversion rate × average order value**. We run it backwards, following the logic of the [sales funnel](/en/blog/what-is-a-sales-funnel/).

> **Illustrative example.** A furniture store in Tashkent sets a December target of 300 million UZS. The figures are for explanation only.

Step by step:

| Stage | Calculation | Result (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue target | — | 300m UZS |
| From repeat customers (30%) | 300 × 0.3 | 90m UZS |
| Needed from new customers | 300 − 90 | 210m UZS |
| Average order value | actual | 3m UZS |
| New sales needed | 210 ÷ 3 | 70 |
| Lead → meeting conversion | actual | 40% |
| Meeting → sale conversion | actual | 30% |
| Lead → sale (overall) | 0.4 × 0.3 | 12% |
| Leads needed | 70 ÷ 0.12 | ≈ 585 |
| Cost per lead | actual | 50,000 UZS |
| Ad budget | 585 × 50,000 | ≈ 29m UZS |

Now hold that number up against reality. If you've been getting about 300 leads a month, doubling the budget may still not get you to 585 — cost per lead tends to rise as you scale. In that case, look at the other levers: conversion and average order value.

Even a few points of conversion make a big difference. In the example above, lifting overall conversion from 12% to 15% means you need roughly 467 leads instead of 585. Our article on [conversion rate](/en/blog/what-is-conversion-rate/) explains how to measure and improve it.

This is also the moment to check how much you can afford to pay for ads: ad spend per sale must not eat your margin. The full calculation is in the article on [unit economics](/en/blog/unit-economics-for-small-business/).

## Step 3: split the plan across reps

Dividing the total equally is the simplest approach, but not always the right one. Consider three factors:

- **capacity** — how many leads one rep can handle well in a month. Use your own data: at what workload did conversion stay stable?
- **experience** — a new rep's first-month target should be lower than a seasoned colleague's;
- **conversion** — it makes sense to give more warm leads to whoever turns them into sales best.

> **Illustrative example.** 3 reps, 585 leads and a target of 70 new sales.

| Rep | Experience | Leads | Conversion (actual) | Sales target |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dilnoza | 2 years | 215 | 14% | 30 |
| Jasur | 1 year | 200 | 12% | 24 |
| Kamola | month 2 | 170 | 9–10% | 16 |
| Total | — | 585 | ≈ 12% | 70 |

Every rep should know their target and how it was calculated. Not "you've got 30", but "we're giving you 215 leads, your conversion is 14%, so that's 30 sales" — that way the plan feels fair. How to measure the gap between reps is covered in [sales rep conversion](/en/blog/sales-rep-conversion/).

## Step 4: break the month into weeks

A monthly plan is too long a horizon. Split it into weekly checkpoints. They don't have to be equal: in many niches demand differs between the start and the end of the month, and December plays by its own rules.

Hold a 20–30 minute meeting every Monday and look at one table:

| Metric | Weekly plan | Actual | Gap | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leads | 146 | 120 | −26 | Review ads with the marketer |
| Meetings | 58 | 55 | −3 | On track |
| Sales | 18 | 12 | −6 | Review the meeting-to-sale stage |
| Average order value | 3m | 2.6m | −0.4m | Check add-on offers |

The figures are illustrative. What matters is one concrete action and one owner for every gap. The table points to the cause: too few leads is a marketing issue; plenty of leads but no sales is a sales issue.

## Step 5: KPIs — outcome and process metrics

If you only watch revenue, you'll see problems late. Split your KPIs into two types.

**Outcome metrics** (visible at month-end):

- revenue and number of sales;
- average order value;
- plan completion, %.

**Process metrics** (visible daily):

- time to first response on a lead;
- number of calls and conversations;
- meetings or consultations booked;
- deals in the CRM with overdue tasks.

Process metrics predict outcomes. If meetings are well below plan on Tuesday, you can expect sales to be short by Friday. Three to five KPIs per rep is plenty; ten metrics scatter attention.

## Motivation: linking the plan to pay

If the plan doesn't affect income, it stays on paper. The most common model is a base salary plus a commission tied to plan completion.

> **Illustrative example.** The commission rate changes with plan completion.

| Plan completion | Commission | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Below 80% | reduced | Base salary and a minimal share |
| 80–99% | standard | Normal performance |
| 100–119% | increased | Target hit |
| 120% and above | increased + bonus | Record month |

A few rules:

- **don't cap it** — if the rate drops after 120%, reps start "parking" deals for next month;
- **pay on payment** — calculate commission when the money arrives, not when the deal closes; otherwise you pay for cancelled orders;
- **set the plan before the month starts** — changing it mid-month destroys trust;
- **add a team bonus** — if the team hits the overall plan, everyone gets a small bonus; it encourages people to help each other.

The right commission rates and base salary depend on your niche, margin and labour market — check them against your financials.

## 5 common mistakes

- **A top-down plan.** When the number reflects a wish rather than data, reps stop taking it seriously in week one.
- **Leads not calculated.** There's a sales target, but it wasn't agreed with marketing — so there aren't enough leads.
- **The same target for everyone.** New reps lose heart, experienced ones coast.
- **Checking only at month-end.** Without weekly tracking, problems show up too late.
- **Ignoring seasonality.** For December, Ramadan or the summer months, look at the same period last year.

## Questions and answers

### What period should a sales plan cover?

The most practical set is an annual goal, a monthly plan and weekly tracking. The annual figure sets direction, the monthly plan links to reps and pay, and weekly tracking flags problems in time.

### What if I have no data from last year?

Spend a month or two simply recording numbers: leads, meetings, sales, average order value. During that time give reps a "guideline" rather than a target. Then build a full plan using your real conversion rates.

### Should reps be penalised for missing the plan?

Find the cause first. If fewer leads came in than planned, that isn't the rep's fault. If there were enough leads but conversion dropped, listen to call recordings, review the script and plan some training. Fines tend to push out precisely the good reps.

### How do the sales plan and the marketing plan connect?

The sales plan tells you how many leads you need, and that becomes the marketing plan and budget. So both teams should build the plan together and review the weekly numbers together.

## Conclusion

Building a sales plan means working backwards from a revenue target: number of sales, leads needed, ad budget. Start from facts, split the plan across reps by capacity and experience, review one table every week, separate outcome and process KPIs, and tie the plan to fair motivation.
