Money goes into ads, leads come in, but sales fall short — a situation many businesses know. The problem is rarely in one place: the profile doesn't inspire trust, ads reach the wrong audience, leads get late replies, and the report doesn't mention sales at all. A marketing audit examines the whole chain and finds where money leaks. This article explains what an audit checks and how to run one yourself.

![When you need an audit](/img/blog/illus/marketing-auditi-1.jpg 1200x675)

## When you need an audit

- ad budget went up but sales didn't;
- cost per lead rises month after month;
- plenty of leads, but "poor quality";
- before starting with a new agency or specialist;
- the business is entering a new stage: a branch, a new product, a new city.

## The 7 blocks of an audit

| Block | Main question |
|---|---|
| 1. Offer and position | Why should a customer choose you? |
| 2. Profile and website | Do visitors trust you and understand the next step? |
| 3. Ad account | Are structure, audiences and budget right? |
| 4. Creatives | Do the first seconds hold attention; is the offer clear? |
| 5. Tracking and analytics | Can you see where each lead came from? |
| 6. Lead handling | How fast and how well are leads answered? |
| 7. Reporting and numbers | Can you see how ads affect sales? |

## Block 1. Offer and position

- Is the offer clear in one sentence: what, for whom, what makes it different?
- Is the difference visible next to competitors?
- Are price and key terms public?

More — [unique selling proposition](/en/blog/unique-selling-proposition/), [competitor analysis](/en/blog/competitor-analysis/).

## Block 2. Profile and website

Check your profile through the eyes of someone seeing it for the first time:

- does the bio make clear in 5 seconds what you do, for whom and how to get in touch;
- highlights: prices, reviews, work, delivery, address;
- the last 9 posts — do they sell, or are they random images;
- are faces and people visible — important for trust;
- if you have a website — does it load fast on a phone, does the form work.

More on profiles — [Instagram profile that sells](/en/blog/instagram-profile-that-sells/).

## Block 3. The ad account

- Are the ad account and Business Manager **in your name**?
- Do campaign objectives match business goals (messages, leads, purchases)?
- Is budget split across too many small ad sets — any "Learning limited" status? More — [Learning limited](/en/blog/meta-learning-limited/).
- Is the audience too narrow or too broad?
- Is retargeting set up?
- Are there rejected ads or warnings?

![Block 4. Creatives](/img/blog/illus/marketing-auditi-2.jpg 1200x675)

## Block 4. Creatives

- How many creatives are running, and when were they last refreshed?
- Is the main idea or product in the first 1–3 seconds? — [Reels hooks](/en/blog/reels-hooks/)
- Do videos make sense without sound — subtitles and on-screen text?
- Are the offer and call to action clear?
- Are A/B tests run? — [A/B testing ad creatives](/en/blog/ab-testing-ad-creatives/)

## Block 5. Tracking and analytics

- Do the pixel and Conversions API work correctly? — [Meta Pixel and Conversions API](/en/blog/meta-pixel-and-conversions-api/)
- Are UTM tags used? — [UTM tags](/en/blog/utm-tags/)
- Is every lead's source recorded in the CRM?
- Does the lead count in the ad account match the CRM?

Without tracking, every other conclusion is guesswork.

## Block 6. Lead handling

This block often reveals the biggest losses. A simple test: submit a lead yourself or through a friend, and watch:

- how long until the first reply;
- whether the question got a direct answer;
- whether the manager offered a next step;
- whether there was a follow-up if you didn't respond.

How reply speed affects sales — [lead response time](/en/blog/lead-response-time/); scripts — [sales script](/en/blog/sales-script/).

## Block 7. Reporting and numbers

- Does the report include spend, leads, cost per lead and **sales**?
- Are cost per sale and ROMI calculated? — [how to calculate ROMI](/en/blog/how-to-calculate-romi/)
- Are numbers compared with last month?
- Is there a concrete plan for next month?

A sample report — [what an ad report should include](/en/blog/what-an-ad-report-should-include/).

## What the audit result should look like

A good audit isn't a 50-page document but a concrete list:

| Problem | Impact | Fix | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leads answered in 3 hours on average (illustrative) | Much of the sales potential lost | An on-duty manager, auto-replies | High |
| Budget split across 8 ad sets | Unstable cost per lead | Merge into 2 ad sets | High |
| No prices on the profile | DMs flooded with questions | "From…" prices in highlights | Medium |
| Creative unchanged for 3 months | Audience fatigue | 3 new videos | Medium |

Fix the high-priority problems that are cheap to solve first.

## Common mistakes

- Limiting the audit to the ad account — the problem is often in sales.
- Ending with a general "it's all bad" and no concrete plan.
- Changing everything at once — then you can't tell what helped.
- Drawing conclusions without tracking.
- Auditing once and forgetting — repeat every quarter.

## Questions and answers

### Can I run the audit myself?

Yes — with the checklist above you'll find the main problems on your own. An outside specialist adds technical issues in the ad account and experience from other industries.

### How long does an audit take?

For a small business, a few days: reviewing the ad account, profile, tracking and sales process. The result is a concrete, prioritised list.

### Do I have to work with an agency after the audit?

No. A good audit's recommendations can be carried out by you or your team. But if you need a team for execution, the audit shows how an agency thinks.

### How often should I audit?

A short audit every quarter; a full audit before big changes.

## Conclusion

A marketing audit checks the whole chain from offer to report: profile, ads, creatives, tracking, lead handling and numbers.
