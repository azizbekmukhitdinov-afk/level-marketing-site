In Uzbekistan, businesses speak at least two languages — Uzbek and Russian — and often English as well. Translating every post, product description and ad into three languages costs time and money. AI translation speeds this up, but literal translation ruins advertising: the text is correct, yet it doesn't connect. Here's how to get quality translation from AI.

## Translation vs localisation

- **Translation** means rendering the words in another language.
- **Localisation** means adapting the text for another audience: tone, examples, wordplay, cultural context.

Ads and posts need localisation. A joke that works in Russian may fall flat in Uzbek, and vice versa. Sometimes it's better to write the text separately for each language — [ad language: Uzbek or Russian](/en/blog/ad-language-uzbek-or-russian/).

## Which texts you can trust to AI

| Fine (with checking) | Use with care | Needs a human translator |
|---|---|---|
| Product descriptions | Ad headlines and slogans | Contracts |
| Internal emails and messages | Brand name and tagline | Official documents and licences |
| Blog article drafts | Humour and wordplay | Medical information about medicines |
| FAQs and help texts | Prices and terms | Legal disclaimers |

Advertising medicines and supplements has its own rules — [medicine and supplement advertising](/en/blog/medicine-supplement-advertising-uzbekistan/).

## A prompt for quality translation

Instead of a bare "translate this", give context:

- **For whom**: "For women aged 25–40 in Tashkent."
- **Where**: "An Instagram post, up to 80 words."
- **Tone**: "Friendly, polite form, not formal."
- **What to keep**: "Don't change the brand name, prices or numbers."
- **Localisation**: "Not word for word — it should sound natural in Uzbek."
- **Options**: "Give 3 headline options."

An illustrative example: "Adapt this Russian Instagram post into Uzbek (Latin script). The audience is young families in Tashkent. The tone is warm and polite. Don't change the price or brand name. Don't translate literally — make it sound natural in Uzbek."

## Latin and Cyrillic

Uzbek is written in two scripts. AI sometimes mixes them or misspells the letters o‘ and g‘. Specify the script in your prompt and check the result. How the two scripts affect search — [Uzbek search: Latin and Cyrillic](/en/blog/uzbek-search-latin-cyrillic/).

## A checking routine

Before publishing an AI translation:

1. **Have a native speaker read it** — does it sound natural?
2. **Numbers and prices** — unchanged?
3. **Brand and product names** — correct?
4. **Terminology** — is that what your industry actually says?
5. **Length** — does it fit the ad format?

For important texts, a back-translation helps: translate the result back into the original language and check the meaning survived.

## A glossary saves time

Build a one-page glossary for your brand: product names, key terms and a list of "how we don't say it". Add it to every prompt, and your translations stay consistent. On brand voice — [what is a brand book](/en/blog/what-is-a-brand-book/).

## The law: language of advertising

Under Article 6 of the Advertising Law, advertising is distributed in the state language and may be repeated in other languages as a translation — and the translation must not distort the core meaning. So the quality of AI translation in ads is a legal matter too — [advertising law in Uzbekistan](/en/blog/advertising-law-uzbekistan/).

## Common mistakes

- Literal translation — the text "reads like a translation".
- Mixing Latin and Cyrillic.
- Publishing without checking.
- Translating contracts and official documents with AI alone.
- A different term every time, with no glossary.

## Conclusion

AI translation is a powerful way to speed up work on business texts, but advertising needs localisation, not translation. Write prompts with context, keep a glossary, specify the script and have a native speaker check every text. Leave contracts and official documents to a human translator.
