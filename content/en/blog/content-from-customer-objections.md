Scrolling competitors' pages for ideas or wondering "what do we post today" is a daily routine for many SMM managers. Yet the strongest content ideas already exist inside your business: in the objections your sales team hears every day. "Too expensive", "I'll think about it", "it's cheaper elsewhere", "I'm not sure about the quality" — each objection is a ready-made post topic. Here's how to turn objections into content systematically.

## Why objections make the best content

An objection is a barrier on the way to purchase. If one customer says it out loud, dozens more think it without saying it — they just don't buy. Content that answers the objection in advance works before the sales conversation even starts:

- people get the answer before they ask the question;
- they arrive at the salesperson already "warm";
- because the content solves a real problem, it gets saved and shared more.

Unlike "About us" posts, this content speaks the customer's language — because it's built from their own words.

## Step 1: collect objections

Objections live in salespeople's heads and get lost every day. Collecting them takes a simple system:

- **15 minutes a week**: the SMM manager talks to sales — what did customers doubt this week, what did they ask, why did they say no?
- **A "reason lost" field in the CRM**: for every lead that didn't become a sale, a reason is selected.
- **DMs and comments**: recurring questions go into one place.
- **Call recordings**: if you have them, listen to 3–5 a week to hear the customer's exact words.

## Step 2: an objection bank

Put the collected objections in a table and group them:

| Group | Objection (in the customer's words) | Times per month | Real reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price | "It's cheaper elsewhere" | 18 | Doesn't see the difference |
| Trust | "I don't know what the quality will be" | 12 | Hasn't seen results |
| Timing | "Not now, later" | 9 | No reason to hurry |
| Process | "How does delivery work?" | 7 | Fear of the unknown |
| Decision | "I'll check with my spouse" | 6 | A family decision |

The numbers are an illustrative example. What matters is frequency: start with the most common objections. The "real reason" column matters too: "too expensive" often means "I didn't understand the difference".

The "I'll consult my family" objection gets its own article — [family purchase decisions](/en/blog/family-purchase-decision/).

## Step 3: 4 formats for each objection

One objection yields several pieces of content. Take "it's cheaper elsewhere" (furniture store, illustrative example):

### Reels: show it

Two sofas side by side: the cheap one and yours. In 30 seconds you show the difference — frame, foam, fabric. Clear even without words.

### Carousel: a comparison table

"What a sofa's price is made of: 5 parts". Each slide covers one component and how it affects lifespan.

### Stories: Q&A

A Stories poll: "What matters more when choosing a sofa: price or lifespan?" Then the answer and explanation. Stories formats — [selling with Stories](/en/blog/selling-with-stories/).

### Customer video

A customer who bought the sofa a year ago shows what it looks like now. That's the strongest answer to an objection — someone else's experience. How to film it — [customer testimonial video](/en/blog/customer-testimonial-video/).

## 3 rules for answering objections

1. **Don't deny the objection — acknowledge it.** "Yes, we're not the cheapest" builds trust. Then explain why.
2. **Answer with proof.** Not claims — demonstrations, numbers, customer experience.
3. **Don't run down competitors.** "Others use poor materials" is risky and erodes trust. Talk only about yourself: what you use and why.

## Connecting content and sales

Objection content works in two directions. First, on the page. Second, in the salesperson's hands: a customer says "too expensive", and the salesperson sends a link to a ready-made Reel. For that:

- salespeople need a list of content for each objection, with links;
- the sales script should include "in this situation, send this video" — see [sales script](/en/blog/sales-script/);
- once a month: which objections became rarer, and which new ones appeared.

## Measuring the result

- **Trends in lost reasons**: does an objection you've covered appear less often in the CRM?
- **Saves and shares**: objection content is usually saved more than ordinary posts.
- **DM questions**: is the same question repeated less often?
- **Sales feedback**: are customers arriving saying "I saw the video"?

## Common mistakes

- The SMM manager guesses objections without asking sales.
- A vague answer: "We do quality work" isn't an answer.
- One post per objection: someone who saw it once forgets a month later. Revisit key objections every quarter.
- Answering price objections only with discounts — see [the discount trap](/en/blog/discount-trap/).

## Questions and answers

### Isn't it risky to raise objections openly in content?

No. People are thinking about them anyway. If you don't answer, they'll look elsewhere for the answer or simply not buy.

### How much objection content per month?

Around a fifth of your content is a good guide. At least one piece a month for each of your 3–5 most common objections.

### What if a small business has no sales team?

Then the owner, or whoever answers DMs, records the objections. Twenty to thirty notes a month give plenty of material.

### Isn't it boring to keep making content about the same objection?

Not if the format changes. Reels, carousel, customer video, Stories — one topic, different forms. And your audience keeps renewing.

## Conclusion

Your best content ideas are in the objections your salespeople hear every day. Collect them, rank them by frequency, answer each with proof in several formats and hand that content to your sales team.
