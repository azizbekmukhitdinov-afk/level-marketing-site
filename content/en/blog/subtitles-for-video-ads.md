Everyone knows a video ad needs subtitles. Yet in many ads the subtitles are there and still unreadable: a line runs across half the screen, the text changes too fast, or it hides under the buttons at the bottom. This article is about the subtitles themselves: line length, time on screen, placement, splitting Uzbek and Russian text, and 7 rules to hand to your editor.

Why subtitles matter and whether to pick a voice-over or live speech, we covered in [sound in video ads](/en/blog/sound-in-video-ads/). Here it is only about subtitle design and text.

![What subtitles are for](/img/blog/illus/videoga-subtitr-qoidalari-1.jpg 1200x675)

## What subtitles are for

Subtitles are a written copy of speech. When someone scrolls the feed with the sound off, subtitles are the only way they follow what is said. So subtitles have one job: **be read at a glance without pulling attention from the shot**.

Two things follow from that:

- **Subtitles run in sync with speech.** Someone talks — text on screen; silence — no text.
- **Subtitles and headlines are different things.** A big on-screen line ("20% off", "Free delivery") carries the offer. Subtitles only repeat the speech. If both sit in the same spot, neither gets read.

## How many characters per line

Film and TV subtitle standards are a good reference. Netflix's Russian style guide, for example, allows up to 42 characters per line and a maximum of 2 lines, and recommends keeping text to one line where possible ([Netflix: Russian Timed Text Style Guide](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/215346638-Russian-Timed-Text-Style-Guide)).

But that is for a horizontal screen. In a vertical Reel on a phone the screen is narrow and the font has to be large. So for ads we recommend shorter lines:

| Format | Characters per line | Lines | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 16:9 (YouTube, website) | 38–42 | 1–2 | Classic subtitles |
| 1:1 and 4:5 (feed) | 30–35 | 1–2 | Larger font |
| 9:16 (Reels, Stories, TikTok) | 20–30 | 1–2 | Short phrases of 2–5 words |

These numbers are our practical recommendation, not a platform requirement. The main test is simple: watch the ad on a phone at arm's length. If you squint, the line is too long or the font too small.

Uzbek words are long: "ko‘rsatkichlarimizni", "buyurtmalaringizni". The same meaning takes more characters than in Russian. So if an Uzbek line fits only 2–3 words, that is normal.

## How long on screen

The second rule is reading speed. For Russian adult programming Netflix sets up to 17 characters per second, and a single subtitle stays on screen for at least 5/6 of a second and at most 7 seconds ([Netflix: Timed Text Style Guide — General Requirements](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/215758617)).

That gives a simple calculation:

| Time on screen | Max characters (17 per second) |
|---|---|
| 1 second | ~17 |
| 2 seconds | ~34 |
| 3 seconds | ~51 |

People watch ads less attentively than films: they are scrolling. So we suggest staying below the limit — 25–30 characters in 2 seconds. If the voice-over is fast, cut the text rather than speeding up the subtitles. To plan how many words fit in advance, see [voiceover script word count](/en/blog/voiceover-script-word-count/).

### Illustrative example: a 15-second ad

Illustrative example: a 15-second Reel for a furniture store in Tashkent, with a 30-word voice-over. We split the subtitles into 6–7 chunks of 2–2.5 seconds each:

1. "Hard to choose a sofa?"
2. "Skip the store trip —"
3. "try it at home"
4. "3 days free"
5. "Don't like it? We pick it up"
6. "Message us via the link in bio"

Each chunk is one idea, 2–5 words. You can read it without looking away from the shot.

## Where to place subtitles: the safe zone

In Reels and Stories the top and bottom of the screen are covered by the interface: profile name, the "Sponsored" label, caption, the "Learn more" button. Subtitles placed there simply won't be seen.

A guideline from Meta's ads guide, repeated by many sources: in vertical Reels ads keep roughly 14% at the top, 35% at the bottom and 6% on each side free of text. Meta updates these figures from time to time, so always check the preview in Ads Manager. More on formats in [ad video sizes](/en/blog/ad-video-sizes/).

In practice this means:

- In a 9:16 ad, put subtitles **slightly below the center of the screen**, not at the very bottom.
- Subtitles must not cover a face or the product. If someone speaks on camera, put the text under the chin, at chest level.
- Keep subtitles **in one spot** for the whole ad. Text that jumps around with each chunk is hard for the eye to follow.

## Font, color and background

Subtitles don't have to be pretty; they have to be readable.

- **Font:** simple, bold, sans-serif, no decoration. Script and thin fonts get lost on a phone.
- **Size:** easy to read at arm's length. When in doubt, go bigger.
- **Color:** white text with a black shadow or a semi-transparent black box. The box keeps it readable in any shot, even against a white wall.
- **Emphasis:** you can highlight one key word in the brand color ("free", "3 days"). Color every word and nothing stands out.
- **Letters:** in Uzbek text, check that o‘ and g‘ render correctly. Some fonts lack the ‘ mark or swap it for another character.

## How to split the text

Well-split subtitles read themselves. Badly split ones make people reread. The principles in Netflix's guide work for ads too: break the line after punctuation and don't separate words that belong together.

- **Don't split linked words:** "soft | sofa", "free | delivery" — keep them on one line.
- **Keep a number with its unit:** "3 days", "200,000 soums" — never across two chunks.
- **End of chunk = end of thought:** "Order today —" and next chunk "we deliver tomorrow" is fine. "Order" | "today we deliver" is not.
- **With two lines,** make the bottom line longer and don't leave one short word on top.

### Uzbek and Russian subtitles

In Tashkent ads are often released in two languages. There are two ways:

| Option | When it fits | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Two separate videos (Uzbek and Russian subtitles) | When you split the ad audience by language | Twice the editing |
| One video with two lines (Uzbek on top, Russian below) | Organic post, bilingual audience | More text, harder to read |

For ads we recommend two separate versions: short subtitles in each audience's own language. Which language to advertise in — [ad language: Uzbek or Russian](/en/blog/ad-language-uzbek-or-russian/).

## Auto-captions: when they are enough

Instagram, CapCut and other apps generate subtitles from speech automatically. It saves time, but in Uzbek there are many mistakes: words are misrecognized, o‘ and g‘ disappear, Russian words get mixed in. So the order is:

1. Generate auto-captions — that's your draft.
2. Check each chunk against the script and fix it by hand.
3. Re-split the chunks using the rules above.
4. Watch the whole video once on a phone with the sound off.

In Meta Ads Manager you can also upload subtitles as a separate SRT file instead of burning them into the video. The file name includes a language code, for example `video.ru_RU.srt` ([Meta for Developers: Video Captions](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/graph-api/reference/video/captions/)). But viewers have to turn such captions on, and they don't show in every placement. For Reels ads it is more reliable to burn subtitles into the video during editing. Which app makes that easy — [video editing apps](/en/blog/video-editing-apps/).

## 7 rules for your editor

Send this list to your editor or SMM specialist:

1. **Speech means subtitles.** No subtitles on music-only shots.
2. **One chunk = one idea, 2–5 words** (in 9:16), 1–2 lines.
3. **At least 1 second on screen,** 2–3 seconds for a longer chunk. Fast voice-over — cut the text.
4. **Position: slightly below center,** inside the safe zone, in one spot for the whole video.
5. **Bold, simple font,** white text with a black shadow or box.
6. **Numbers with units and linked words stay on one line.**
7. **Proofreading:** o‘ and g‘, names and prices match the script.

And the final check: on a phone, sound off, one full viewing. If you can't follow what is said on that viewing, neither will your customer. Want to compare subtitle versions? See [A/B testing ad creatives](/en/blog/ab-testing-ad-creatives/).

## Questions and answers

### Do ads fail without subtitles?

A video with no speech (only music and on-screen text) works without subtitles. But if someone speaks or there is a voice-over, a viewer with the sound off won't follow it and will miss the offer.

### What about word-by-word (karaoke) subtitles?

This style is common in Reels and can hold attention. But if single words change too fast, they are hard to read. If you use it, keep chunks of 2–4 words and highlight only the word being spoken.

### Should Uzbek subtitles be in Latin or Cyrillic script?

It depends on the audience. Young people and most of the ad audience read Latin script. Whatever you choose, don't mix scripts within one video.

### Can you trust auto-translated subtitles?

As a draft, yes; as final text, no. Someone who knows the language should check the translation against the script, especially chunks with prices, deadlines and terms.

## Conclusion

Good subtitles go unnoticed — the viewer simply understands what is said. That takes little: short chunks, enough time, one spot inside the safe zone, a bold font and a sound-off check on a phone. Agree on these 7 rules with your editor once, and every new video will be readable by default.
