"We work at 50%" is something you hear in almost every shop. But 50% of what: markup or margin? The difference sounds technical, yet the money left from a single sale can differ several times over. In this article we separate the two ideas with formulas, a conversion table and an illustrative example in soums, then show how discounts and promotions hit your margin.

## Two ideas, the same numbers

Markup and margin are calculated from the same two numbers: the cost and the selling price. The only difference is what you divide by.

- **Markup** is the amount added to the cost, as a percentage **of the cost**.
- **Margin** (gross margin) is the amount left from the price, as a percentage **of the selling price**.

Illustrative example: a product costs 100,000 soums and sells for 150,000 soums. The difference is 50,000 soums.

- Markup: 50,000 ÷ 100,000 = **50%**.
- Margin: 50,000 ÷ 150,000 = **33.3%**.

The same 50,000 soums, two different percentages. So whenever someone says "50%", ask which one they mean.

## All the formulas in one table

| What you calculate | Formula |
|---|---|
| Markup, % | (price − cost) ÷ cost × 100 |
| Margin, % | (price − cost) ÷ price × 100 |
| Price from markup | cost × (1 + markup) |
| Price from margin | cost ÷ (1 − margin) |
| Markup to margin | markup ÷ (1 + markup) |
| Margin to markup | margin ÷ (1 − margin) |

The most useful row is "price from margin". If you want a 40% margin, you divide the cost by 0.6, you do not multiply it by 1.4. 100,000 ÷ 0.6 = 166,667 soums, while 100,000 × 1.4 is only 140,000 soums. That gap of 26,667 soums on every unit is the price of the confusion.

## Markup to margin conversion table

It is worth printing this table and putting it next to whoever sets prices.

| Markup | Margin |
|---|---|
| 10% | 9.1% |
| 20% | 16.7% |
| 25% | 20.0% |
| 30% | 23.1% |
| 40% | 28.6% |
| 50% | 33.3% |
| 60% | 37.5% |
| 75% | 42.9% |
| 100% | 50.0% |
| 150% | 60.0% |
| 200% | 66.7% |
| 300% | 75.0% |

Two conclusions follow. First, margin can never reach 100%, while markup can be 300% or 500%. Second, the bigger the markup, the further apart the two numbers get. "Doubling the price" (100% markup) is only a 50% margin.

## Why owners mix them up

The reason is simple: different people in a business speak different languages.

- **Suppliers and sales staff** usually think in markup: "add 30% to the purchase price".
- **Accountants and financial statements** work with margin: profit in reports is shown as a share of revenue.
- **The owner**, when planning, says "we need 30% profit" without saying which percentage.

As a result, the plan is built on a 30% margin while prices are set with a 30% markup. Illustrative example: cost 100,000 soums, with a 30% markup the price is 130,000 and the real margin is 23.1%. For a 30% margin the price should have been 142,857 soums. Every unit loses 12,857 soums. At 2,000 units a month that is 25.7 million soums, the salary of one or two employees.

## What goes into cost

Most mistakes are not in the formula but in the word "cost" itself. Some people only count what they paid the supplier. The right approach is to include everything spent until the product is ready to sell.

| Level | What you subtract | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Gross margin | Purchase price, freight to warehouse, customs, packaging | What the product itself leaves you |
| Contribution margin | Plus: card fees, delivery to customer, marketplace commission, ad cost per order | How much each sale contributes to fixed costs |
| Operating profit | Plus: rent, salaries, utilities, software | What the business actually earned |

Gross margin alone is not enough for pricing. Ads and delivery come out of every sale, so calculate contribution margin too. When it covers your fixed costs is shown by the [break-even point](/en/blog/break-even-point/), and how much you can pay to acquire a customer is answered by [unit economics](/en/blog/unit-economics-for-small-business/).

## How a discount eats your margin

A discount comes off the price, but the cost stays the same. So a 10% discount does not take 10% of your profit, it takes much more. The question is: how much must sales volume grow to earn the same gross profit after the discount?

Formula: **required volume growth = discount ÷ (margin − discount)**.

| Margin | 5% discount | 10% discount | 15% discount | 20% discount |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20% | +33% | +100% | +300% | profit drops to zero |
| 30% | +20% | +50% | +100% | +200% |
| 40% | +14% | +33% | +60% | +100% |
| 50% | +11% | +25% | +43% | +67% |

A shop with a 30% margin that gives 20% off has to sell three times as much to earn the same. Look at this table before launching any promotion. More in [the discount trap](/en/blog/discount-trap/) and [Black Friday for business](/en/blog/black-friday-for-business/). A bonus toward the next purchase often eats less margin than a straight discount, see [cashback marketing](/en/blog/cashback-marketing/).

There is a flip side. If you raise prices by 5% with a 30% margin, gross profit stays the same even if sales fall by up to 14.3% (formula: increase ÷ (margin + increase)). That is a good argument for not being afraid of price reviews, see [how to raise prices](/en/blog/how-to-raise-prices/).

## Illustrative example: a clothing store

The numbers are illustrative and only show the order of the calculation.

| Item | Normal day | 30% off |
|---|---|---|
| Selling price | 400,000 | 280,000 |
| Cost (purchase + freight + customs) | 200,000 | 200,000 |
| Gross profit | 200,000 | 80,000 |
| Gross margin | 50% | 28.6% |
| Card fee (3%) | 12,000 | 8,400 |
| Packaging | 5,000 | 5,000 |
| Ad cost per sale | 60,000 | 60,000 |
| **Contribution margin** | **123,000** | **6,600** |

The owner thinks: "I double the price, 100% markup, good money." In reality, on a normal day each sale leaves 123,000 soums toward fixed costs, 30.8% of the price. A 30% discount drops that to 6,600 soums. To cover rent and salaries during the promotion, the store would need to sell 18 times as much. A promotion like that fills the till but brings no profit.

In this example a free accessory with two items, or a bundle that raises the average order, would work better than a discount. On how buyers perceive price, see [pricing psychology](/en/blog/pricing-psychology/).

## Working rules

- **Use one language in every document.** In the plan, price list and report, write "margin" or "markup" explicitly.
- **Calculate price from margin:** price = cost ÷ (1 − target margin).
- **Set a margin floor.** For example: "any discount that takes contribution margin below 15% needs the owner's approval".
- **Give managers a floor, not the cost.** They should know which discount is allowed, not the purchase price.
- **Treat advertising as a cost of sale.** Take ad spend per sale from your [CAC](/en/blog/customer-acquisition-cost-cac/) and subtract it from contribution margin.
- **Recheck monthly.** If supplier prices or the exchange rate change, the old markup no longer gives the old margin.

## Common mistakes

- Setting a "30% profit" markup while the plan is built on margin.
- Counting only the purchase price as cost and forgetting freight and customs.
- Subtracting the discount from the margin ("20% margin, 10% off, 10% left") without calculating how much volume must grow.
- Calling a product profitable without counting ads, card fees and returns.
- Running the whole store on one average margin. Some items bring profit, others only bring turnover.

## Questions and answers

### Which matters more: margin or markup?

For managing the business, margin matters more: it shows what share of revenue stays with you and matches your reports. Markup is handy for setting a price quickly at the till. You can use both, just never mix them.

### What is a normal margin?

There is no universal number. Margin depends on the industry, how fast stock turns over and your fixed costs: a fast-moving product can live on a low margin, a slow one needs a high margin. The better question is different: at what volume does this margin cover fixed costs and advertising? The break-even point answers that.

### Can margin be above 100%?

No. Margin is a share of the price, and profit cannot be larger than the price. Markup has no limit: if a product costing 100,000 soums sells for 500,000, the markup is 400% and the margin is 80%.

### How do I calculate with VAT?

The main rule is to take price and cost on the same basis: both without VAT or both with VAT. If you mix them, the margin comes out wrong. Check the tax details with your accountant.

## Conclusion

Markup is calculated from cost, margin from price. That single difference sits behind many mistakes in plans, promotions and ad budgets. Set prices from your target margin, check the volume table before any discount, and add advertising to the cost of every sale.
