A customer asks "Do you deliver?" and gets one answer in the morning and a different one in the evening. One manager says "yes, for free", another says "depends on the address". To the customer it is one brand; inside it is three different people. A **saved replies library** fixes this: your team answers the most common questions the same way, correctly and in your brand voice. Here is how to build one, which 15 questions to start with and how to keep it up to date.

## What a saved replies library is and why you need one

A saved replies library is a set of the questions customers ask most often in Instagram DMs, Telegram and on the phone, with agreed answers to each. It is not an auto-reply and not a bot. A person reads it, then copies the answer or adapts it to the conversation.

The library gives you three things:

- **Consistency.** Everyone says the same thing about price, timing, warranty and delivery. No customer will say "but your colleague told me something else".
- **Speed.** Managers don't invent an answer from scratch each time. How response speed affects sales is covered in [lead response time](/en/blog/lead-response-time/).
- **A training manual.** A new manager answers correctly in their first week instead of running to the boss with every question.

Whether your brand voice stays on paper or works every day depends on this library. How to describe your voice is in [brand tone of voice](/en/blog/brand-tone-of-voice/); the library carries those rules into daily conversations.

## Step 1: collect real questions

Don't make questions up — take them from your chats. An hour or two is enough:

1. **Open the last 100 conversations in Instagram DMs and Telegram.** Write the first message of each into a spreadsheet.
2. **Ask your managers:** "Which question do you hear more than 5 times a day?" Include whoever answers the phone.
3. **Check the comments.** The same questions repeat under your ads.
4. **Merge similar ones.** "How much?", "price?", "send the price list" — one question.
5. **Sort by frequency.** The 15 most frequent make the first version of the library.

Price, address, timing, payment and delivery usually top the list. Answering the price question is a topic of its own — see [replying to "How much?"](/en/blog/replying-to-how-much/).

## 15 questions to start with

The list below is a starting point for most businesses. Swap some of them for your niche.

| # | Question | What the answer must include |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | How much is it? | Price or range + what it depends on + a clarifying question |
| 2 | Where are you located? | Landmark, map link, parking |
| 3 | What are your hours? | Days and hours, holiday schedule |
| 4 | Do you deliver? | Area, cost, timing |
| 5 | How long will it take? | A realistic timeframe and what it depends on |
| 6 | Do you offer instalments? | Partners and terms (real ones only) |
| 7 | Is there a warranty? | Length and what it covers |
| 8 | Can I return or exchange it? | Terms and deadlines |
| 9 | Any discounts? | The current offer, or an honest "not right now" |
| 10 | Is the item in the photo in stock? | How to check, an extra photo or video |
| 11 | Can I choose the size or colour? | Options, how to order |
| 12 | Can I see it first? | Showroom, trial lesson, consultation |
| 13 | Why are you more expensive? | Value: materials, service, warranty — without naming competitors |
| 14 | How do I order? | 2–3 steps and the next action |
| 15 | Complaint: late, broken, didn't like it | Apology, a clarifying question, resolution time, the person responsible |

Number 15 is special: you write a reply for complaints, but never send it word for word. For complaints the library holds a **procedure**: what to say, who to hand over to and how fast to respond. Replying to public reviews is covered in [online reputation and reviews](/en/blog/online-reputation-and-reviews/).

## Step 2: put each answer on a card

One question, one card. When every card has the same structure, the library is easy to read and update.

**Card template:**

- **Question** — how customers ask it (2–3 versions).
- **Short reply (DMs, Telegram)** — 1–3 sentences, ending with one clarifying question.
- **Spoken reply (phone)** — the same meaning in words that are easy to say.
- **Second-language version** — Uzbek or Russian, if customers write in both.
- **What we don't say** — banned words and promises.
- **Who we hand over to** — when the question goes beyond the library.
- **Last checked** — date and owner.

**Illustrative example: a furniture store in Tashkent, question 4 "Do you deliver?"**

> **DM:** "Yes, we deliver across Tashkent. The cost depends on the district — which district are you in? I'll calculate it and send you the exact amount."
>
> **Phone:** "Yes, we deliver. Which district do you live in? I'll tell you the price right now."
>
> **What we don't say:** "Sure, we'll bring it tomorrow" — until the warehouse and the van confirm.
>
> **Who we hand over to:** delivery to the regions — the logistics manager.

The text above is an illustrative example; figures and terms in your business will differ.

## Writing in your brand voice: 5 rules

A correct answer is not enough — it has to sound like your brand.

1. **One form of address.** Formal or informal "you" — the same across the whole library. For most businesses, formal is the safer choice.
2. **Short sentences.** Replies are read on a phone. If one runs longer than 3 sentences, split it into two messages.
3. **Every reply ends with a question.** "Which district are you in?", "Which size do you need?" — the conversation keeps going.
4. **A specific number, not a vague promise.** Not "soon" but "3–5 business days".
5. **An agreed amount of emoji and exclamation marks.** If one manager puts three "!" in every sentence, the brand sounds different.

| Not this | This |
|---|---|
| "Price in DM" | "From 2.4 million sum, depending on size. Which size do you need?" *(illustrative example)* |
| "I don't know, I'll check" | "I'll check and message you within 15 minutes" |
| "Everything's amazing here!!!" | "12-month warranty, including the mechanism" *(illustrative example)* |

## Step 3: where to keep the library

The library only works if it is at the manager's fingertips, not in the boss's folder.

- **One master copy.** A Google Doc or a Notion page. One or two people can edit, the whole team can read.
- **Quick replies in Telegram.** Telegram Business lets you save preset replies under a short name and call them up quickly in a chat; the feature comes with a Telegram Premium subscription ([official Telegram documentation](https://core.telegram.org/api/business)). Setup is covered in [Telegram Business](/en/blog/telegram-business-features/).
- **Saved replies in Instagram and Facebook.** The Meta Business Suite inbox also lets you save replies. Menu names change from time to time, so check Meta's Help Center before setting them up. Auto-replies are covered in [automated responses in Meta Business Suite](/en/blog/meta-business-suite-automated-responses/).
- **A printed sheet for the phone.** The 5 most common questions and spoken replies, on the manager's desk.

The key rule: saved replies in apps are copied from the master. The library changes first, then the copies in Telegram and Meta.

## Step 4: an update routine

An outdated reply is worse than none: the customer hears last month's price or an expired offer. So the library needs an owner and a schedule.

| When | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|
| A price, offer or term changes | The relevant cards are updated the same day | Library owner |
| Every week | Managers note new recurring questions | Managers |
| Every month | 5–10 conversations are read at random: was the library reply used, did the customer write back after it | Head of sales or senior manager |
| Every quarter | Unused cards are removed, new ones added | Library owner |
| Before holidays and peak season | Hours, delivery and timing are rechecked | Library owner |

If every card shows a "last checked" date, outdated ones stand out at a glance.

## Common mistakes

- **The boss writes the library alone.** Managers don't use replies that aren't in their own words. Write it together.
- **Replies are too long.** Nobody reads a 6-sentence reply in a DM.
- **The reply doesn't end with a question.** The customer says "thanks" and leaves.
- **Bilingual customers are forgotten.** Someone writing in Uzbek gets a Russian template.
- **It's written once and forgotten.** Three months later half of it is wrong.

## Questions and answers

### Won't saved replies sound robotic?

They will if you send them word for word without a name. Use the library as a base: add the customer's name, a detail from their question and one clarifying question.

### How many questions should I start with?

Fifteen is enough; they usually cover most daily conversations. After that, managers add new ones every week.

### Is a replies library the same as a sales script?

No. A script sets the order of a conversation: greeting, discovering needs, offer, close. A library holds ready answers to individual questions. They complement each other — more on scripts in [sales script](/en/blog/sales-script/).

### Does a small business with one manager need one?

Yes. If the manager is ill or on holiday, the customer still gets the same answer from whoever covers. And when a new hire joins, the library is ready.

## Conclusion

A saved replies library is the most practical form of a brand voice. Collect 15 questions from real chats, write a short DM reply and a spoken reply for each, note what not to say, keep one master copy and review it monthly. Then whoever the customer talks to, they hear one brand.
