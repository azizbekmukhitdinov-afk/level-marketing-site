Your ads worked well for two weeks, then the cost per lead started creeping up — even though the audience, budget and offer stayed the same. The usual culprit is creative fatigue: people have seen the same video or image so many times that they no longer notice it. Here's how to spot creative fatigue, tell it apart from other problems, and replace creatives systematically.

![What creative fatigue is](/img/blog/illus/kreativ-charchashi-1.jpg 1200x675)

## What creative fatigue is

**Creative fatigue** is the drop in an ad's performance after it has been shown to the same audience many times. The first time someone sees it, they stop; the second time, they're curious; by the fifth, their thumb scrolls past on autopilot.

For the ad system it looks like this: fewer people click, fewer leave a lead, so every result costs more. Meta tracks this separately: Ads Manager can show a warning that interest in a creative is declining, and Meta has a [help page with creative fatigue recommendations](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/1346816142327858).

Important: fatigue doesn't mean the ad is bad. Quite the opposite — the best creative often fatigues first, because the system showed it the most.

## 5 signs of creative fatigue

Don't judge by a single number. Fatigue usually shows up when several metrics get worse at the same time.

| Sign | Where to look in Ads Manager | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency is rising | Frequency column | How many times, on average, one person saw the ad. Formula: impressions ÷ reach |
| CTR is falling | CTR (link click-through rate) | A smaller share of viewers clicks |
| Cost per result is rising | Cost per result | Leads or messages got more expensive |
| Fewer video views | 3-second video plays, ThruPlay | The hook no longer stops people |
| A warning appears | Delivery column | Meta itself detected fatigue |

The classic picture: frequency climbs week after week, CTR drops and cost per lead goes up. If only the cost rises while frequency and CTR stay flat, the cause may be elsewhere (see the next section).

Another simple sign is comments. When you see "this ad is everywhere" or "this video again", the audience is tired of the creative.

There's no single frequency norm for every business. As a practical guide (illustrative example): if weekly frequency in ads to new audiences passes 3–4 and the cost per lead starts rising at the same time, it's time to check the creative. In retargeting, frequency is naturally higher.

## Fatigue or something else: how to tell

Not every rise in cost per lead is about the creative. Before replacing ads, answer 4 questions:

1. **Did you change anything recently?** Changing the budget, audience or optimisation event sends the ad set back into learning, and results are unstable for a few days. More on this in our article on [learning limited](/en/blog/meta-learning-limited/).
2. **Has the season changed?** Before holidays, auction competition grows and ads get more expensive for everyone. In that case CTR usually holds steady while CPM (cost per 1,000 impressions) rises.
3. **Do the website and form work?** If there are clicks but no leads, the problem is in the next step, not the creative.
4. **Do leads turn into sales?** If Ads Manager looks fine but sales don't come, run through the check in [why ads don't bring sales](/en/blog/why-ads-dont-bring-sales/).

A short rule: **frequency up and CTR down — it's the creative. CPM up and CTR flat — it's the auction or the season.**

## When fatigue comes faster

The same creative can last two months in one business and burn out in two weeks in another. Speed depends on:

- **A small audience.** Retargeting, one district or a narrow niche — few people, so each one sees the ad many times sooner.
- **A large budget relative to the audience.** The more you spend per day, the more often the system has to show the ad to the same people.
- **One creative per ad set.** The system has no choice — all impressions go to a single ad.
- **Creatives that look too alike.** Versions that differ only in background colour or button text are perceived as one ad.

For example, a beauty salon in Chilonzor advertising within a 3 km radius has a much smaller audience than an online store selling across all of Tashkent — it needs to be ready to refresh creatives more often.

## How to replace creatives: 6 steps

### 1. Check once a week

Not daily — once a week, on the same day. For each active ad, compare frequency, CTR and cost per result with the previous week. Daily swings are normal; don't make decisions based on them.

### 2. Don't switch off what works — add something new

When you see signs of fatigue, you don't have to turn the old ad off right away. First add 1–2 new creatives to the ad set and let the system redistribute impressions. Switch off the old ad only once it stops delivering results.

### 3. Choose what to refresh

You don't need to shoot a new video from scratch every time. Choose based on how fatigued the ad is:

| Level | What to do | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | Change the hook and first frame | A different question or shot in the first 3 seconds |
| Medium | Change the format | A carousel instead of a video, or the other way round |
| Strong | A new angle (idea) | Instead of price — the problem, a customer story or the process |

Changing the hook is the cheapest route: you can edit the opening of one video in 3–4 versions. Ready-made ideas are in [10 Reels hooks](/en/blog/reels-hooks/).

### 4. Keep a reserve of creatives

Starting a new creative once fatigue has set in is too late. Always have 2–4 creatives ready but not yet launched. To get there, plan one shoot a month for several ads at once. The process is described in [how ad creatives are produced](/en/blog/how-ad-creatives-are-produced/).

### 5. Add changes in one batch

Add new creatives all at once during the weekly check, not one per day. The ad set goes back into learning less often, and results are easier to compare.

### 6. Keep a log

A simple table: creative name, launch date, idea, how many days until it fatigued, average cost per lead. After 2–3 months you'll see how long a creative typically "lives" in your business and which ideas last longer. That's the most valuable data for planning. To know for sure which version is better, use [A/B testing of ad creatives](/en/blog/ab-testing-ad-creatives/).

## Illustrative example: a furniture store

A furniture store in Tashkent advertised kitchens with two videos (illustrative example, numbers for explanation only):

| Week | Frequency | CTR | Cost per lead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | 1.6 | 1.4% | $4 |
| Week 2 | 2.4 | 1.2% | $4.5 |
| Week 3 | 3.5 | 0.8% | $6.5 |

In week three all three signs got worse at once — that's fatigue. The store didn't switch the videos off; it added two new creatives from its reserve: the same video with a new hook ("Is your kitchen 6 square metres?") and a new idea showing a measurer visiting a home to take measurements. The following week, most impressions shifted to the new creatives.

## Weekly checklist

1. Has each active ad's frequency gone up compared with last week?
2. Has CTR dropped?
3. Has cost per result risen?
4. Is there a warning in the Delivery column?
5. Are there comments like "this ad again"?
6. Did the budget, audience or other settings change recently?
7. How many ready creatives are in reserve?
8. Are this week's numbers in the log?

## Questions and answers

### How often should creatives be replaced?

There's no fixed period — numbers decide, not the calendar. With a large audience a creative can work for several months; with a small one it can fatigue in two weeks. That's why a weekly check beats a "refresh every month" rule.

### Can a fatigued creative be used again later?

Often, yes. After a break of a month or two, or with a different audience (for example, a new city), an old strong creative can work again. That's another reason to keep a log.

### How many creatives should an ad set have?

For a small business, 3–5 genuinely different creatives per ad set is a good start. One will fatigue quickly, while 15 will spread a small budget too thin.

### Is changing the copy enough?

Rarely. In the feed people see the image or video first and read the text later. When fatigue hits, refresh the visual and the first seconds first.

## Conclusion

Creative fatigue is a natural stage in the life of every ad: you can't avoid it, but you can manage it. Check frequency, CTR and cost per lead once a week, separate fatigue from seasonality and settings mistakes, keep ready creatives in reserve and add new ones in batches. That way your cost per lead won't suddenly jump. At Level Marketing we track creatives every week in this order and plan shoots in advance.
