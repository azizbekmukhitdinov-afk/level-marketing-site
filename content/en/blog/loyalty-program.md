Winning a new customer is often far more expensive than bringing back an existing one, and as ads get pricier, the gap grows. A loyalty programme gives customers a concrete reason to return: cashback, points, tiers or a members' club. But a poorly built programme becomes nothing more than a discount expense. Here's how to build one properly.

## Types of programme

| Type | How it works | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|
| Cashback / bonus points | A percentage of each purchase as points for the next one | Cafés, shops, beauty salons |
| Stamp card | "6th coffee free" | Frequent purchases: coffee, car washes |
| Tiers | Bigger spenders reach higher tiers with more perks | Clothing, cosmetics, online stores |
| Members' club / subscription | A monthly fee for ongoing perks | Fitness, delivery, services |
| Referral bonus | Bring a friend and both get a bonus | Almost everyone |

## Economics first

Before launching, work out:

- **Margin**: 5% cashback comes out of your margin — [unit economics](/en/blog/unit-economics-for-small-business/).
- **Purchase frequency**: how often a year does a customer return? If once a year, cashback will have little effect.
- **Required uplift**: how much the programme must increase repeat purchases to pay for itself.

An illustrative example: average order 100,000 soum, margin 40%. 5% cashback costs 5,000 per purchase. If the programme brings a customer back 5 times a year instead of 4, the extra purchase adds 40,000 in margin — more than the cashback costs.

## Value isn't only money

The strongest programmes offer more than discounts:

- early access to new products;
- a birthday gift;
- a free service (delivery, gift-wrapping, diagnostics);
- a personal manager or no-queue service.

These perks cost less margin and create a sense of being special.

## The technical side: start simple

- **A Telegram bot**: sign-up, points balance, notifications — [a Telegram bot for leads](/en/blog/telegram-bot-for-leads/).
- **A CRM**: each customer's purchases and points — [CRM for small business](/en/blog/crm-for-small-business/).
- **Phone number as the ID**: no need for a plastic card.
- **Till or POS integration**, if possible.

Consent and legal requirements when handling customer data — [customer database and personal data](/en/blog/customer-database-personal-data-uzbekistan/).

## A launch plan

1. Set the goal: repeat purchases, order value or referrals.
2. Choose one simple mechanic — complex rules confuse customers.
3. Train staff to offer the programme to every buyer.
4. Announce it to existing customers, starting them with bonus points.
5. After 3 months, review: has the share of returning customers changed?

## Common mistakes

- Complicated rules: "points expire after 30 days, Tuesdays only".
- Staff don't mention the programme.
- Points are hard to redeem.
- The programme exists, but customers never hear from you.
- Results aren't measured.

## What to measure

- The share of customers in the programme.
- Return frequency: members vs non-members.
- Average order value and LTV — [repeat sales and LTV](/en/blog/repeat-sales-and-ltv/).
- Programme costs (cashback, gifts) vs the extra margin.

## Questions and answers

### Does a small business need a loyalty programme?

If customers return regularly, yes. Start with a simple stamp card or a Telegram bot.

### How much cashback should I offer?

It depends on margin and purchase frequency. A small percentage plus extra perks often works better than large cashback.

### Should points expire?

A reasonable expiry (say 6–12 months) encourages people to return; one that's too short annoys them.
