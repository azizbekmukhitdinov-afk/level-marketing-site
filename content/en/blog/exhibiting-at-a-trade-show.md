Stand rental, a banner, flyers, a team on its feet for three days: exhibiting at a trade show is not cheap. Yet many companies come back with a box of business cards that ends up in a desk drawer. This guide covers how to prepare, how to collect contacts on the stand and, above all, what to do in the 7 days after the show.

![Exhibiting at a trade show: four questions before you apply](/img/blog/illus/korgazmada-qatnashish-1.jpg 1200x675)

## Exhibiting at a trade show: four questions before you apply

Tashkent hosts a trade show almost every week. The Uzexpocentre website lists more than ten events for November and December 2026: food industry, packaging, agriculture, beauty, children's goods and others ([uzexpocentre.uz](https://uzexpocentre.uz/)). Dates can change, so always check them on the organiser's site.

Answer four questions before you send the application:

1. **Who visits?** Ask the organiser for last year's visitor profile: specialists, buyers, dealers or the general public. If your customer is not among them, a beautiful stand will not help.
2. **Why are we going?** Pick one main goal. "Everything at once" cannot be measured.
3. **Can the product be shown?** A show suits things people can touch and try: furniture, equipment, building materials, food.
4. **Who will process the contacts, and when?** If there is no answer, postpone until next year.

| Goal | What you do at the show | How you measure it |
|---|---|---|
| Find dealers and wholesale buyers | Explain terms, book meetings | Meetings booked, contracts |
| Sell to end customers | Show the product, take orders | Orders and deposits |
| Launch a new product | Hand out samples, collect opinions | People who tried it, feedback collected |
| Study the market | Walk the competitors' stands | A table of prices and offers |

More on working with dealers in [Marketing for manufacturers](/en/blog/marketing-for-manufacturers/).

## Goal and maths: what one contact costs

A trade show is an advertising channel like Instagram ads. Count it the same way: how much you spent, how many contacts you got, how many became sales.

Include every cost, not only the stand:

- participation fee and floor space;
- stand construction, banner, furniture, lighting;
- flyers, catalogues, samples, gifts;
- staff time (they are not doing their usual work on show days);
- transport, meals and, in another city, a hotel.

> **Illustrative example.** A furniture manufacturer in Tashkent spent 60 million soums in total on a three-day show. The stand collected 150 contacts, 30 of them "hot": a clear need and a deadline. Six contracts were signed within two months. One contact cost 400,000 soums and one contract cost 10 million. The figures are invented to show the method; run the maths on your own numbers.

Do this calculation **before** the show, in reverse: how much profit one contract brings, how many contracts cover the costs, how many hot contacts that requires. That number is the team's plan for the three days. To compare the show with other channels, use the [customer acquisition cost](/en/blog/customer-acquisition-cost-cac/) formula.

## Preparation: 30 days before the show

Much of the result is decided before the doors open.

| When | What gets done |
|---|---|
| 30 days out | Goal and target number. Stand layout. Rota: who is on the stand on which day |
| 3 weeks out | Flyers and catalogues go to print. QR codes and links are ready. The contact form is tested |
| 2 weeks out | Invitations to current customers and dormant leads: "We'll be on the stand on this date, come by" |
| 1 week out | Announcement on Instagram and Telegram: hall, stand number, what you will show. Team rehearsal |
| 1 day out | Stand assembly, internet and chargers checked, drafts of the "next 7 days" messages ready |

Two items deserve extra attention.

**Invitations.** The most valuable visitor is the one you invited yourself. For a customer whose negotiation has stalled, the stand is a convenient reason to meet: they see the product and talk to the owner.

**Team rehearsal.** Everyone on the stand should know three things: the opening line, the three questions to ask and where the contact is recorded. Role-play it at least once.

## The stand: the three-second rule

A visitor walking down the aisle looks at a stand for about three seconds. In that time they need to understand one thing: **what is sold here and for whom**.

- **The top line is the product, not the logo.** "Kitchen furniture direct from the manufacturer" works better than an unfamiliar brand name.
- **One hero product in the centre.** Do not bring the whole range. Let one strong item stand out; the rest lives in the catalogue.
- **Let people touch it.** A sample, a cut of the material, a machine that is running. People remember what they held.
- **The table does not block the entrance.** When staff sit behind a table, the stand looks like a closed shop. Move the table to the side and keep a person close to the aisle.
- **Phone in hand only to record a contact.** Nobody approaches someone staring at a screen.

Do not open with "Can I help you?" The answer is "No, thanks", and they walk on. Ask an open question: "Are you looking for a shop or for yourself?"

## Flyers and handouts: fewer, but traceable

Visitors leave with a bag of paper and read little of it. So keep the flyer short and ask for **one action**.

- Front: who it is for and what you offer, one image.
- Back: a show-only offer with its deadline, a QR code, a phone number.
- Use a separate QR code for the show, with a UTM tag in the link. Later you will see how many people reached your site or bot from the show. The setup is covered in [How to measure offline advertising](/en/blog/measure-offline-advertising/) and [UTM tags](/en/blog/utm-tags/).
- Do not hand the expensive catalogue to everyone. Give it after a hot conversation or send a digital copy after the show, which is one more reason to get in touch.

## Collecting contacts: three methods and A/B/C grading

A business card is not a contact. It does not say what the person asked about, when they want to buy or who they spoke to. Three days later nobody remembers which of the 150 cards matter.

**Three methods:**

1. **A short form on a phone** (Google Forms or a CRM form). The team member fills it in within 30 seconds of the conversation. The most reliable method.
2. **A Telegram bot behind a QR code.** The visitor scans it and gets the catalogue; you get the contact. Handy when there is a queue. Setup is covered in [Collecting leads with a Telegram bot](/en/blog/telegram-bot-for-leads/).
3. **Card plus note.** Photograph the card and add a comment to the form straight away. Keep paper forms as a backup in case the internet drops.

Five or six fields are enough: name, company and role, phone, what they were interested in, when they need it, grade (A/B/C). Add the team member's name at the end.

| Grade | Signs | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| A, hot | Clear need, deadline and a decision-maker | Call within 24 hours, meeting or quote |
| B, warm | Interested, timing unclear | Personal message and materials within 2–3 days |
| C, cold | Just looked, took a flyer | General thank-you message, then an invitation to your channel |

**On consent.** A phone number is personal data. When you take a contact, say what it is for and tick a box in the form: "Agrees to receive offers and news". In Uzbekistan, advertising SMS has required the recipient's consent since 1 November 2026. Details are in [Customer database and personal data in Uzbekistan](/en/blog/customer-database-personal-data-uzbekistan/) and [SMS advertising rules in Uzbekistan](/en/blog/sms-advertising-rules-uzbekistan/).

Set aside 15 minutes every evening: number of contacts, the list of A-grade contacts, what to change tomorrow. Send A-grade contacts a short message that same evening; do not wait for the show to end.

## The 7-day plan after the show

The money spent on the show is recovered or lost in this week. A visitor saw dozens of stands in three days and will not remember you a week later.

| Day | What gets done |
|---|---|
| Day 1 | Every contact is in the CRM with the source "trade show, November 2026". Calls to A-grade contacts. A short thank-you message to everyone |
| Day 2 | A-grade contacts get a quote or a proposal. B-grade contacts get a personal message answering what they asked |
| Day 3 | Calls to B-grade contacts. Promised samples and catalogues are sent |
| Day 4 | Photo and video report from the show on Instagram and Telegram. C-grade contacts get a channel invitation |
| Day 5 | Second attempt for A and B contacts who did not answer, through another channel (no answer by phone, message on Telegram) |
| Day 6 | Meetings and demos. Every contact's status is updated in the CRM |
| Day 7 | The count: costs, contacts, meetings, sales. A 30-minute review with the team |

The first message should not be a generic "Thank you for visiting our stand". Refer to the conversation: "Aziz, yesterday at the stand we discussed a 12-metre kitchen. Here is the quote for three options, as promised." This is why the form needs the "what they were interested in" field. How to build the offer is covered in [Commercial proposal template](/en/blog/commercial-proposal-template/); why speed matters, in [Lead response time](/en/blog/lead-response-time/).

Do not close the books on day 7. With expensive products and B2B sales a deal takes months, so return to the table after 30 and 90 days. Those numbers decide whether you exhibit next year.

## 6 trade show mistakes

1. **Going without a goal.** "Everyone is exhibiting" is not a reason. Without a goal and a target number the result cannot be judged.
2. **Collecting only business cards.** A card without a note becomes a stranger's piece of paper within a week.
3. **Putting random staff on the stand.** Someone who does not know the product says "a manager will call you", and the visitor moves to the next stand.
4. **Bringing everything.** Out of ten products, none is remembered.
5. **Postponing the follow-up.** After the show the team is tired and work has piled up, so contacts wait two weeks and go cold. Set the 7-day plan and its owner in advance.
6. **Counting only part of the cost.** If you count only the stand rental, the show looks cheap and cannot be compared with other channels.

## Questions and answers

**Does exhibiting pay off for a small business?**
It depends on the niche and the price of the product. When one contract brings a large profit (equipment, furniture, wholesale), a few deals can cover the costs. With low-priced goods, sales from the stand alone rarely do. Run the reverse calculation and ask the organiser for the visitor profile before deciding.

**Is it worth going without a stand, as a visitor?**
Yes. In the first year it is often the better choice: you see competitors' offers, meet suppliers and dealers, and learn which hall and what kind of stand you would need next year.

**How many people should be on the stand?**
On a small stand, at least two at any time: while one is talking, the other greets the next visitor. The days are long, so draw up a rota. Tired staff sell worse.

**Are gifts and prize draws worth it?**
A gift draws a crowd, but not always customers. Give it to people who filled in the short form or had a conversation, not to everyone. If you run a prize draw, publish the terms in writing in advance; we covered this in [Instagram giveaway rules in Uzbekistan](/en/blog/instagram-giveaway-rules-uzbekistan/).

**What if we have no CRM?**
Google Sheets is enough: one contact per row, with columns for grade, owner, next step and its date. What matters is that every contact sits in one place and has an owner.

## Conclusion

Exhibiting at a trade show has three parts: preparation, three days of work and the week after. Most companies put their effort into the middle part. The result comes from the first and the third: a clear goal, invited customers, contacts with notes and a personal reply to each within 7 days. Before the show, build one table with costs, targets and owners. When you return, check yourself against it.
