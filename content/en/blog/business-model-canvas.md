Ask business owners about their business model and many say, "We sell a product." But a business model is the full picture: for whom, what value, through which channels and how the business makes money. The handiest tool is the **Business Model Canvas**: a single page of 9 blocks. Alexander Osterwalder proposed it, and it became popular through *Business Model Generation* (2010), which he wrote with Yves Pigneur.

## The 9 blocks of the canvas

1. **Customer segments** — whom we serve — [market segmentation](/en/blog/market-segmentation/).
2. **Value proposition** — which customer problem we solve — [unique selling proposition](/en/blog/unique-selling-proposition/).
3. **Channels** — how customers find us and buy.
4. **Customer relationships** — personal service, self-service, community.
5. **Revenue streams** — how we get paid: one-off sales, subscriptions, commissions.
6. **Key resources** — people, equipment, brand, data.
7. **Key activities** — production, sales, service.
8. **Key partners** — suppliers and partners.
9. **Cost structure** — fixed and variable costs.

## A filled-in example (illustrative example — a family bakery)

- **Segments**: local families and offices.
- **Value**: hot bread every morning by 7:00, delivery to offices.
- **Channels**: the shop, Telegram orders, maps — [add your business to maps](/en/blog/add-your-business-to-maps/).
- **Relationships**: a list of regulars, an office subscription.
- **Revenue**: retail plus a monthly office subscription.
- **Resources**: a tandoor oven, bakers, the location.
- **Activities**: baking, delivery, taking orders.
- **Partners**: a flour supplier, a courier.
- **Costs**: flour, wages, rent, utilities.

On one page it's clear: the office subscription is a stable income stream and the growth opportunity.

## Common model types

- **One-off sales** — a shop or a service.
- **Subscription** — monthly payments, stable revenue.
- **Freemium** — a free basic tier, paid extras.
- **Marketplace / commission** — connecting sellers and buyers.
- **Franchise** — selling the brand and the system.

## Finding weak spots

Ask a question of each block:

- Is the segment clear and big enough?
- Does the value proposition differ from competitors'? — [competitive advantage](/en/blog/competitive-advantage/)
- Is the cost of acquiring a customer lower than their value? — [unit economics](/en/blog/unit-economics-for-small-business/)
- Are you overly dependent on one partner or channel?

## Linking it to marketing

The canvas is the foundation of a marketing plan: segments are your audience, the value is your ad message, channels are your ad platforms, and revenue and costs set the budget limits — [planning a marketing budget](/en/blog/planning-a-marketing-budget/).

## Questions and answers

### Does the canvas replace a business plan?

No. The canvas is a quick-thinking tool; a business plan is a detailed document — [the marketing section of a business plan](/en/blog/business-plan-marketing-section/).

### How long does it take to fill in?

A first version takes 1–2 hours. Then you test it in conversations with customers.

### Does an established business need one?

Yes — to spot growth opportunities and risks.
