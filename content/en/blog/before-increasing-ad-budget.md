Sales drop, so the owner raises the ad budget. No result, so they switch agencies. The new agency doesn't deliver either — more budget, another switch. Three times a year the verdict is "ads don't work". Yet in many cases the ads did work: they brought people in. The money leaked somewhere else — in the offer, the price or the first call. This article covers what to check before raising the budget, and when raising it really is the right move.

![Ads are a multiplier, not a creator](/img/blog/illus/reklama-byudjetini-oshirishdan-oldin-1.jpg 1200x675)

## Ads are a multiplier, not a creator

Sales can be written as a simple formula:

**Sales = flow of people × conversion to sale × average order value**

Ads only increase the first factor — the flow of people. If conversion is close to zero, doubling, tripling or growing the flow tenfold won't change the outcome. Zero times anything is zero. It's just a more expensive zero.

### Illustrative example: same budget, two outcomes

| | Business A | Business B |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly ad budget | 20,000,000 so‘m | 20,000,000 so‘m |
| Leads | 400 | 400 |
| Lead to sale | 2% | 12% |
| Sales | 8 | 48 |
| Cost per sale | 2,500,000 so‘m | 417,000 so‘m |

(Illustrative example.) The ads performed identically for both. The difference is in what happens after the ad. If Business A doubles its budget, it gets 16 sales — but the cost per sale stays the same. Fixing conversion first is far cheaper.

## A familiar loop: budget → agency → "ads don't work"

Why the cycle repeats:

- **The visible number is ad spend.** It's in the ad account, the report, the bank statement — so it's what people look at first.
- **The invisible number is lost customers.** Unanswered messages, people who left saying "I'll think about it", people who went quiet after hearing the price — none of it gets recorded.
- **A new agency also starts with ads.** Creatives and audiences change, but the offer and sales process stay the same.

So the money leaks from the same place every time — only each time someone different gets the blame.

## 7 checks before raising the budget

### 1. Do people who came on their own buy?

The simplest and most important test. Out of 10 people who came via referral, maps or as returning customers, how many buy? If even "warm" customers don't buy, the problem isn't the ads — it's the offer, price or sales. Ads bring colder people, and the results with them will be lower still.

### 2. Is the offer clear in one sentence?

What, for whom, and why it's better than competitors — can you say it in one sentence? If your offer is "quality service, affordable prices", people can't tell you apart and will pick the cheapest — [unique selling proposition](/en/blog/unique-selling-proposition/).

### 3. Does the price fit the market?

Compare your price with competitors and count the share of people who "left after the price". If every second conversation ends at the price, that's a pricing or value-explanation problem — not an ads problem.

### 4. What was the first reply like?

Submit a lead yourself or through a friend. How many minutes until a reply? Was the question answered directly? Was a next step offered? In many businesses the biggest losses happen right here — [lead response time](/en/blog/lead-response-time/), [sales script](/en/blog/sales-script/).

### 5. Can you see where each sale came from?

Without tracking, raising the budget is a blind decision. The CRM should record the source; UTMs and the pixel should work. Otherwise you can't tell which campaign earns money and which loses it.

### 6. How much can you pay for one customer?

Profit per order × lead-to-sale conversion = maximum cost per lead. If your current cost per lead is above that line, a bigger budget grows the loss — [unit economics](/en/blog/unit-economics-for-small-business/).

### 7. Is the team ready for more leads?

If leads double, who answers them? If managers can't keep up already, the extra leads will go unanswered — and cost per sale will rise along with cost per lead.

## When raising the budget is right

Signs that a bigger budget will genuinely pay off:

- cost per lead has been stable for several weeks;
- lead-to-sale conversion is known and healthy;
- cost per sale is below your unit-economics limit;
- the sales team can handle extra leads;
- campaigns aren't stuck in "Learning limited" — [Learning limited](/en/blog/meta-learning-limited/).

Raise the budget in steps rather than big jumps, and watch cost per sale after each step. If it spikes, stop and find out why.

## How to find where the money leaks

A simple one-week check:

1. Gather every lead from the last month in a CRM or spreadsheet.
2. Give each a status: no reply, conversation, asked the price, bought, declined + reason.
3. Count at which stage the most people drop off.
4. Fix that stage and watch for 2–3 weeks.
5. Only then decide about the budget.

## Common mistakes

- Raising the budget as the first response to falling sales.
- Switching agency or ads specialist without changing the offer or sales.
- Watching only cost per lead, not cost per sale.
- Never measuring conversion of customers who "came on their own".
- Raising the budget without warning the sales team.

## Questions and answers

### If I stop the ads, won't sales fall even further?

They might. That's why the recommendation isn't to stop ads, but to keep them at the current level and fix conversion. Once that's fixed, every so‘m works harder.

### How do I know whether the problem is the ads or sales?

Compare customers who came on their own with customers from ads. If neither group buys, the problem is the offer or sales. If only ad customers don't buy, it's the audience or what the ad promises.

### How often can I raise the budget?

When results are stable and, after each increase, you've gathered enough data to judge cost per sale. Sudden big jumps often make cost per lead unstable.

### What if the agency suggests raising the budget?

Ask the 7 questions above. A good agency will show you conversion to sale and unit economics first, and talk about budget after.

## Conclusion

Ads don't create sales — they multiply what's already there. If even people who came on their own don't buy, a bigger budget only buys a more expensive zero.
