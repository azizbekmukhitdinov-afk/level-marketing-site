There is a field called "Bid strategy" in Ads Manager. Most people never touch it, and some set a limit straight away "to make leads cheaper", after which the ads stop delivering. This article compares Meta bid strategies: what each one does, how they differ, and which one a small business should pick.

## What a bid strategy is

Every impression on Meta is an auction. Your ad competes with other advertisers for one person's attention. A bid strategy tells the system how to bid in that auction on your behalf.

In its [Business Help Center](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/1619591734742116), Meta groups the strategies into three types:

- **budget-based** — the main aim is to spend the full budget and get the most results or the most value;
- **goal-based** — you set a cost or ROAS goal and the system tries to stay at that level;
- **manual** — you set the maximum bid for the auction yourself.

The setting usually sits at the ad set level, or at the campaign level when a campaign budget is on. Menu names and locations change from time to time, so remember the logic, not the button.

One note: older guides use the names Lowest cost, Cost cap and Target cost. Ads Manager uses different names now, although media buyers still say "cost cap" out of habit. The current names are below.

## Meta bid strategies: 5 options in one table

| Strategy | What the system does | What you provide | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Highest volume | Spends the full budget and gets as many results as it can | Budget only | Almost everyone, especially a new campaign |
| Highest value | Steers the budget towards higher-value purchases | Budget and purchase value data | An online store with varied prices |
| Cost per result goal | Tries to keep the average cost per result around your figure | Budget and a cost goal | A campaign with stable results and history |
| ROAS goal | Tries to keep return on ad spend at a set level | Budget and a ROAS goal | An online store that sends purchase values |
| Bid cap | Never bids above your figure in the auction | Budget and a maximum bid | An experienced team that can model conversion rates |

Now each one in turn.

## Highest volume: the starting point for most

This is the default for most performance goals. The system spends your whole budget and tries to get the most results for that money. It makes no promise on cost: when the market gets more expensive, so do your leads.

Meta's own example is an event organiser who wants as many people as possible at a festival and does not mind what one attendance costs.

**Strength:** nothing holds the algorithm back, the budget is spent, and the [learning phase](/en/blog/meta-learning-limited/) passes faster.

**Weakness:** the auction sets the price, not you. In competitive months such as December, the cost per result can rise.

For a small business that is acceptable. On a small budget, the most reliable way to control cost is not the strategy but the [creative, the offer and lead quality](/en/blog/cost-per-lead-cpl-how-to-lower/).

## Cost per result goal: holding the price

Here you give the system a cost goal, for example "a lead should cost about this much on average". The system tries to get as many results as possible around that level.

There are three things to know.

**1. It is an average goal, not a ceiling.** Meta says so directly: strict adherence to the cost goal is not guaranteed. Some days results come in above it and some days below.

**2. The budget may not be fully spent.** [Meta's recommendations](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/176339196621566) say that under high competition the system may spend less of your budget, because it is holding cost, not volume. If the goal is set too low, the ads barely deliver.

**3. It needs data.** Meta says the strategy works best when an ad set gets 50 to 100 or more conversions a week.

Meta's own recommendations:

- start with a goal 10–20% above your average cost per result. Meta's example: if a purchase cost $20 over the last 30 days, set the goal at $22;
- with no history, run Highest volume for at least 2 weeks first and take the cost from reporting as your starting value;
- judge performance on weekly averages, not daily figures;
- do not change the goal often: after a change, wait at least 7 days;
- if a large share of the budget is left unspent, raise the goal by 5–10%.

## Bid cap: why small businesses usually don't need it

Bid cap is manual control. You set the maximum bid for the auction and the system does not go above it.

This is where most of the confusion comes from. A bid cap is not a cost per result. As [Meta explains](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/272503946776144), it is the maximum bid for a predicted action, and the action itself is not guaranteed. A $5 bid cap does not mean "a lead will never cost more than $5".

Meta itself says that if you want to control your costs, Cost per result goal is the better choice. Bid cap suits advertisers who clearly understand how a maximum bid relates to the results in reporting.

In a small business, bid cap often ends the same way: the bid is set low "to make it cheaper", the ad loses auctions, the budget is not spent, and the owner concludes that advertising doesn't work.

## Highest value and ROAS goal: for online stores

These two strategies look at purchase value, not the number of leads. For them to work, the system has to know the value of every purchase, so the [Pixel and Conversions API](/en/blog/meta-pixel-and-conversions-api/) must send the purchase event with its amount.

- **Highest value** spends the full budget and focuses on higher-value purchases.
- **ROAS goal** tries to hold return at a set level. Meta's example: with a $100 budget and a wish for about $110 in purchases, the ROAS goal is 1.100. This is not a guarantee either.

If you sell through Direct messages or a lead form, you don't need these two: the system cannot see the purchase amount. In that case you calculate return yourself, [using ROMI](/en/blog/how-to-calculate-romi/).

## Which strategy to pick: 4 questions

| Question | Answer | Choice |
|---|---|---|
| Is the campaign new, or is there no results history? | Yes | Highest volume, for at least 2 weeks |
| Does the ad set get fewer than 50 results a week? | Yes | Stay on Highest volume |
| Are results stable at 50–100+ a week, and do you know your profitable cost? | Yes | Test Cost per result goal |
| Online store with purchase values sent to the system? | Yes | Highest value or ROAS goal |

Bid cap is left out of the table on purpose. It needs separate calculations and experience.

**Illustrative example.** A furniture store in Tashkent collects leads through a lead form. It spends $20 a day, gets about 30 leads a week, and the average cost per lead is $4.50. The owner wants to set a cost per result goal: "I'll bring the lead down to $3".

There are two problems. First, 30 leads a week is below the 50–100 Meta recommends. Second, $3 is far below the average cost, while Meta advises starting 10–20% above it, at roughly $5–5.40. With a $3 goal, delivery will most likely slow down. The better route is to stay on Highest volume, merge ad sets and lower the cost with [fresh creatives](/en/blog/creative-fatigue/). The numbers are for illustration only and are not real client data.

## 5 bid strategy mistakes

1. **Setting the cost goal from wishful thinking.** The goal starts from your real average cost, not from what you would like.
2. **Treating bid cap as cost per result.** It is a ceiling on the bid, not the price of a lead.
3. **Changing it every day.** Switching strategy or goal is a significant edit for the algorithm. After a change, wait a week.
4. **Using a cost goal at low volume.** With 10–15 results a week, the system doesn't have enough data.
5. **Calling unspent budget a saving.** If the budget isn't spent, the leads aren't coming either. Find the cause first: a low goal, a narrow audience or a tired creative.

One more point: don't change the strategy on the same day you [increase the budget](/en/blog/before-increasing-ad-budget/). Otherwise you won't know which change affected the result.

## Questions and answers

### Which bid strategy gives the cheapest lead?

None of them. Highest volume tries to get the most results for your budget, but the market sets the price. Cost per result goal holds the price, but volume can drop. A cheap lead comes from the offer and the creative, not from the strategy.

### I set a cost per result goal and the ads stopped. Why?

Most often the goal is below the real cost. The system can't find enough auctions at that price and doesn't spend the budget. Raise the goal by 5–10% and watch for a week, or go back to Highest volume.

### Which strategy should I use on $10–20 a day?

Highest volume. On that budget it is hard to reach 50–100 results a week, so a cost goal won't work consistently.

### Does switching strategy restart learning?

Many guides list a bid strategy change among significant edits. So do it on schedule, no more than once a week, and judge the result after 7 days.

## Conclusion

Meta bid strategies are not a magic button but an instruction to the system. For most small businesses the right order is to start with Highest volume, learn your real average cost, and test a cost goal carefully only once volume is sufficient.
