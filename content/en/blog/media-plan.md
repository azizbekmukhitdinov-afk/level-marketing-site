A **media plan** is a table that shows which channel your ad money goes to, when, how much, and what you expect in return. Without one, advertising runs on "500 dollars on Instagram this month, we'll see", and at the end of the month nobody knows what worked. This article explains what a media plan is, how to build it and how to do the maths, using an illustrative example.

## What a media plan is and why you need one

A media plan is the numerical plan of an ad campaign. It usually contains:

- **goal** — leads, sales, awareness or visits;
- **audience** — who sees the ads, in which city or region;
- **timing** — campaign start and end, broken into weeks;
- **channels and formats** — Instagram, Google, Telegram, YouTube, banners and so on;
- **budget** — per channel and per month;
- **forecast** — impressions, reach, clicks, leads;
- **KPIs** — what each channel is judged by.

A media plan is neither a marketing strategy nor a marketing budget. Strategy answers "what and for whom", the budget says "how much in total", and the media plan splits that money across channels and days and estimates the result in advance. How to set the overall amount is covered in [planning a marketing budget](/en/blog/planning-a-marketing-budget/).

A media plan gives you three things:

1. **Clear expectations.** At the start of the month the owner knows roughly how many leads to expect.
2. **Something to compare against.** At the end of the month, plan and actuals sit side by side.
3. **Money does not get stuck in one channel.** Each channel's role and limits are written down in advance.

## The metrics in a media plan

Before the maths, let's agree on terms.

| Metric | Meaning | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Impressions | How many times the ad was shown | Budget ÷ CPM × 1000 |
| Reach | How many unique people saw it | Impressions ÷ frequency |
| Frequency | How many times one person saw it on average | Impressions ÷ reach |
| CPM | Cost per 1,000 impressions | Budget ÷ impressions × 1000 |
| CTR | Share of viewers who clicked | Clicks ÷ impressions |
| CPC | Cost per click | Budget ÷ clicks |
| CR | Share of visitors who left a request | Leads ÷ clicks |
| CPL | Cost per lead | Budget ÷ leads |

The difference between reach and impressions is explained in [a separate article](/en/blog/reach-vs-impressions/), and what drives the cost of 1,000 impressions is in [what is CPM](/en/blog/what-is-cpm/).

There is one core chain: **budget → impressions → clicks → leads → sales**. A media plan puts an estimated number on each link. Where do the numbers come from? Ideally, from your own ad account statistics for previous months. If there are none, the first month becomes a test month and the plan gets sharper from month two.

## How to build a media plan: 7 steps

### 1. Start from the business goal

Not "more leads" but a number: "40 sales a month". Then work backwards. If 10% of leads turn into sales, you need 400 leads. That number tells you how much money advertising needs.

### 2. Describe the audience and geography

Who buys, what age, which city, which language. Prices and messages often differ between Tashkent and the regions, so it is better to give them separate rows in the plan.

### 3. Choose channels by their job

Each channel has its own role:

- **search ads** — capture existing demand: people are already looking;
- **Instagram and Facebook** — create demand and collect leads;
- **video and reach campaigns** — build awareness;
- **Telegram channels** — reach narrow audiences;
- **retargeting** — brings back people who visited or messaged but did not buy.

### 4. Split the budget

A simple rule: most of the money goes to channels that have proven themselves, a smaller share to tests. For example, 70% to core channels, 20% to growing channels, 10% to new experiments. This is a starting point, not a law.

### 5. Write a forecast for each channel

State the pricing model (CPM, CPC or CPL) and the expected price, then calculate the volume. It is more honest to forecast a range than a single figure: "250–350 leads".

### 6. Set frequency and timing

One impression rarely convinces anyone, but fifteen are annoying. What frequency is normal is covered in [ad frequency](/en/blog/ad-frequency/). Break the timeline into weeks and mark holiday, seasonal and promotion weeks.

### 7. Agree on KPIs and reporting

One main metric and a reporting date for each channel. For example: "Every Monday we compare plan and actuals; at month end we update the media plan."

## Media plan template: an illustrative example

A one-month media plan for a furniture store in Tashkent. **All numbers are illustrative** — they show how the calculation works; prices in your niche will differ.

| Channel | Job | Budget | Model | Illustrative price | Expected volume | Main KPI |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Instagram/Facebook, lead form | Leads | $900 | CPL | $3 | ~300 leads | Cost per lead, share of qualified leads |
| Instagram, reach video | Awareness | $300 | CPM | $1.5 | 200,000 impressions, ~50,000 reach | Reach, frequency, video views |
| Google Search | Existing demand | $500 | CPC | $0.25 | ~2,000 clicks | Site leads, cost per lead |
| Telegram channels | Test | $200 | Per post | $50 / post | 4 posts | UTM visits, leads |
| Retargeting | Bring back | $100 | CPM | $3 | ~33,000 impressions | Return visits, leads |
| **Total** | | **$2,000** | | | | |

How it was calculated:

- reach video: $300 ÷ $1.5 × 1000 = 200,000 impressions; at a frequency of 4, reach is 200,000 ÷ 4 = 50,000 people;
- Google Search: $500 ÷ $0.25 = 2,000 clicks; with a 3% site conversion rate (illustrative), about 60 leads;
- lead form: $900 ÷ $3 = 300 leads.

Add "plan" and "actual" columns. During the month the actual column fills up, and you immediately see which channel is falling behind.

> A media plan is a forecast, not a promise. It starts to work when you update it with real numbers at the end of the first month.

## KPIs for each channel

Judging every channel by a single KPI is the most common mistake. Asking a reach video for a low cost per lead is as wrong as asking search ads for reach.

| Channel type | Main KPI | Secondary KPIs |
|---|---|---|
| Lead form, lead campaign | Cost per lead (CPL) | Share of qualified leads, lead-to-sale rate |
| Search ads | Cost per lead or call | CTR, conversion rate, Quality Score |
| Reach and video | Reach and CPM | Frequency, video completion |
| Telegram channels | Cost per visit or lead | Cost per subscriber, post views |
| Retargeting | Conversion of returning users | Frequency, cost per sale |

A list of key marketing and sales metrics is in [what is a KPI](/en/blog/what-is-kpi/). Put only 1–2 main KPIs into the media plan, or the report turns into a pile of numbers.

## Spreading the budget over time

You do not have to split money evenly across the month. There are three common approaches:

- **continuous** — the same budget every day; for niches with steady demand all year (clinics, car services);
- **pulsing** — a base level plus boosts in certain weeks; for promotions, holidays or seasons;
- **burst** — a big budget for a short period; an opening, a new product, the run-up to an event.

Show this in the media plan with weekly rows: week 1 — $400, week 2 — $400, week 3 (promotion) — $700, week 4 — $500 (illustrative). Then a budget change in the ad account is a planned decision, not an accident.

## Common mistakes

- **A media plan without a goal.** The table looks good, but it does not answer "how many sales do we need".
- **Numbers pulled from thin air.** An "average CPM" from the internet may not fit your niche. Use your own statistics or plan a test month.
- **All the money in one channel.** If the channel stops or the ad account is blocked, the flow drops to zero.
- **Expecting leads from a reach campaign.** Each channel is judged by its own job.
- **Plan and actuals are never compared.** The media plan is made and filed away.
- **The sales team is ignored.** 300 leads arrive and nobody answers them — it is the process that fails, not the ads.

## Questions and answers

### Does a small business need a media plan?

Yes, in a simple form. Even with one channel and a budget of a few hundred dollars, three rows are enough: how much we spend, how many leads we expect, what one lead costs. At the end of the month those three numbers tell you whether the advertising worked.

### What period does a media plan cover?

Usually one month, with an overview for the quarter or season. A monthly plan is updated at the end of each month: working channels get more money, weak ones get cut. Annual media plans are mostly for large brands and awareness campaigns.

### Where do I get numbers if I'm advertising for the first time?

Plan the first month as a test: split the budget across 2–3 channels, write a minimum KPI for each and, at month end, record the real CPM, CPC and cost per lead. The second month's plan is built on those numbers and is far more accurate.

### Can I keep a media plan in Excel?

Of course. A simple Google Sheets or Excel table is enough: channels in rows, budget, price, volume and KPI in columns, with formulas. The key is filling in the "actual" column every week.

## Conclusion

A media plan is a table that splits ad money across channels and weeks and estimates the result in advance. Work backwards from the business goal, pick channels by their job, split the budget between core channels and tests, give each channel its own KPI and compare plan with actuals every week. The first month's numbers will be estimates — from month two, the plan rests on real data.
