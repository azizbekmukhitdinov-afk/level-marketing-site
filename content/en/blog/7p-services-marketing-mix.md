A clinic, a training centre, a beauty salon or an agency puts money into ads, leads arrive, yet customers do not come back and do not recommend. Often the problem is not the advertising: a service is delivered by people, it happens as a process, and the customer cannot "touch" it in advance. The classic 4Ps do not fully cover this, which is why services use the **7Ps** model. Below we go through each P with practical questions, a checklist and a worked example.

## From 4Ps to 7Ps: where the model comes from

The 4P form of the marketing mix (product, price, place and promotion) was proposed by E. Jerome McCarthy in 1960. In 1981 Bernard Booms and Mary Jo Bitner proposed adding three more elements for service firms: **people, process and physical evidence**. The history and definitions are summarised in the [marketing mix article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketing_mix).

If the 4P basics are new to you, start with [the 4Ps of marketing](/en/blog/4p-marketing-mix/); this article builds on that foundation.

## Why services need three more Ps

A service differs from a physical product in four important ways, and each one explains a new P:

- **You cannot inspect a service in advance.** You hold a phone in the shop, but you judge a dentist's work only afterwards. So customers look for trust signals: physical evidence.
- **A service is produced and consumed at the same time.** The customer is inside the process: the queue, the call, the wait are all part of the experience. That is process.
- **Quality depends on the person.** Two stylists in the same salon give two different results. That is people.
- **A service cannot be stored.** An empty slot is lost for good, so booking and reminders turn directly into money.

## The 7Ps in one table

| P | Key question for a service | Example (language school) |
|---|---|---|
| Product: the service | What exact result does the customer get? | A programme to reach conversational level in 3 months |
| Price | How is the price explained through value? | Monthly payment, first lesson free |
| Place | Where and in what format is it delivered? | A branch plus an online group |
| Promotion | Which channel and which message? | Paid social ads, a lead form |
| People | Who delivers the service and how are they trained? | Teacher, receptionist, sales manager |
| Process | What does the customer go through step by step? | Enquiry → call → placement test → group |
| Physical evidence | What does the customer judge quality by? | Classroom, certificates, student videos |

A quick note on the first four: we covered how to justify a service price in [how to set prices](/en/blog/how-to-set-prices/), and how to make the first purchase easier in [the entry offer](/en/blog/entry-offer/). Below we focus on the three new Ps.

## People

In a service business, the employee is the company as far as the customer is concerned. If the receptionist is rude on the phone, even the best doctor cannot save the situation.

What to check:

- **A list of every role that touches the customer**: who takes calls, who answers Instagram messages, the specialist, the cashier.
- **A standard for each role**: greeting, response time, how to state the price, how to handle objections.
- **Training**: what a new hire learns before facing customers.
- **Whether motivation is tied to the customer's result.** At Level Marketing, for instance, specialists' bonuses are tied to the client renewing the contract, so people work for the long-term result rather than for one month.

Detailed rules for staff behaviour: [customer service standards](/en/blog/customer-service-standards/).

## Process

Process is the set of steps a customer goes through from first contact to result. If it is not written down, every employee does it their own way.

How to build the process:

1. Split the customer path into steps: enquiry, first reply, booking, visit, service, payment, follow-up.
2. Assign an owner and a deadline to each step.
3. Measure where customers drop off most often; this is the logic of a [sales funnel](/en/blog/what-is-a-sales-funnel/).
4. Fix the one step with the biggest loss, then move to the next.

As an example, Level Marketing splits its work into six steps: brief and point A → strategy (plan versus actual) → content → launch → optimisation → report and point B. The client always knows which stage they are at, and in services that is a large part of trust.

The places where a process leaks the most money: a slow reply to enquiries, no reminder after booking, and losing contact after the service.

## Physical evidence

Since customers cannot see a service in advance, they judge its quality by the signals around it. That is physical evidence:

- **The premises**: entrance, cleanliness, waiting area, signage.
- **The online shop window**: Instagram profile, website, map listing, photo quality.
- **Documents**: contract, receipt, warranty card, report.
- **Traces of results**: before-and-after photos (within the law), customer videos, reviews; see more in [social proof in ads](/en/blog/social-proof-in-ads/).
- **Staff appearance**: uniform, badge, the way they speak.

For an agency, physical evidence is the report. Level Marketing presents its monthly report in the client's office, turning an invisible "ad setup" into concrete numbers and a meeting.

## A 7P checklist for a service business

Answer each question with "yes / partly / no". The P with the most "no" answers is where to start.

| P | Question |
|---|---|
| Service | Is the customer's result written in one sentence? |
| Service | Do packages have clear limits (what is included and what is not)? |
| Price | Is the price stated the same way on the site and in reply templates? |
| Place | Do address and opening hours match on maps, the site and Instagram? |
| Promotion | Are lead volume and cost measured per channel? |
| People | Is there a written standard for every customer-facing role? |
| People | Are new hires trained before they face customers? |
| Process | Is there a set, measured response time for enquiries? |
| Process | Do booked customers get a reminder? |
| Process | Do you ask for feedback after the service? |
| Evidence | Are there real photos and reviews on the profile and site? |
| Evidence | Does the customer keep a document (contract, report, warranty)? |

## Worked example: a dental clinic (illustrative example)

A clinic spends 4 million soums a month on ads and gets 200 enquiries. The ads "work", but the owner is unhappy with revenue. A 7P review showed that enquiries get a reply only after several hours on average (process), booked patients get no reminder (process), and the receptionist gives no clear answer when asked about price (people).

Three changes were made: a reply within 30 minutes, a reminder the day before the appointment, and a reply template for price questions. The ad budget and number of enquiries stayed the same.

| Metric | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Enquiries | 200 | 200 |
| Booking rate | 30% (60 people) | 40% (80 people) |
| Show-up rate | 70% (42 people) | 85% (68 people) |
| Ad budget | 4,000,000 soums | 4,000,000 soums |
| Cost per patient who showed up | about 95,200 soums | about 58,800 soums |

The numbers are made up and show the logic: without adding a single soum to ads, fixing people and process alone raised the number of patients who came in from 42 to 68. That is why, in a service business, checking the last three Ps is cheaper than raising the budget.

## Common mistakes

- **Filling in the 7Ps only for a presentation.** The table exists, but no decision changed. Each P should produce one concrete task.
- **Blaming everything on ads.** If the cost per lead is fine but sales are not, the cause is usually people or process.
- **Writing a standard and never checking it.** Without listening to calls and measuring response time, a standard is forgotten within a month.
- **Treating physical evidence as interior design only.** Your online profile and documents are evidence too, and many customers first "see" you there.

## Questions and answers

### Are the 7Ps only for service businesses?

The model was built for services, but it is useful for product businesses too: the shop assistant, the delivery process and the packaging are all part of the customer experience. In a pure product business the main weight simply stays on the 4Ps.

### Which P should I start with?

Start with the numbers. Many leads but few sales: people and process. Few leads: promotion and the offer itself. Customers not returning: the after-service part of the process and physical evidence.

### How often should I run a 7P review?

A full review once or twice a year makes sense, plus whenever you open a new branch or launch a new service. Process metrics such as response time, booking rate and show-up rate are worth checking weekly.

### How do I bring customer feedback into the 7Ps?

Run a short survey after the service and sort the answers by P: "the specialist was polite" is people, "I waited a long time" is process. NPS is a convenient tool for measuring loyalty.

## Conclusion

The 7Ps are the 4Ps plus three parts of a service: who delivers it, what process the customer goes through, and what they judge quality by. In a service business the fastest growth often comes not from new ads but from putting these three Ps in order. Fill in the checklist, find the weakest P and measure one change in numbers.
