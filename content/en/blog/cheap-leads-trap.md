The report looks great: cost per lead is down, lead volume is up. Yet at the end of the month there's no more money in the till. The sales team says "the leads are poor"; the ads specialist says "our cost per lead is the lowest on the market". Both are right. The trouble is that cheap leads often bring cheap customers — or people who will never become customers at all. This article explains how the cheap leads trap works and how to get out of it.

## Why a cheap lead isn't always good

An ad platform does the job you give it. If the job is "the cheapest possible lead", the algorithm finds the people most easily persuaded to fill in a form. They aren't always the people who buy the most.

Cheap leads often come from:

- **the curious** — "just checking the price";
- **freebie hunters** — when the ad says "free";
- **the wrong audience** — another city, no budget, a different service needed;
- **accidental taps** — a pre-filled form goes out in one tap.

## The "red ocean": when everyone says the same thing

The second reason runs deeper. If your ad says what competitors say — "quality, fast, affordable" — the customer is left with one way to choose: price. You're swimming in a "red ocean": everyone fights for the same customer with the same promise, and whoever sets the lowest price wins.

In a market like that:

- cost per lead can be low, because ads go to a broad audience;
- but profit per sale shrinks, because you sell at a discount;
- customers aren't loyal — next time they'll find it cheaper elsewhere.

## Positioning: a pricier lead, a bigger order

The way out is a clear answer to "why you". With a clear position, your ads talk not to everyone but to the people who suit you. Leads may cost a little more, but:

- these customers are ready to pay more;
- they haggle less over price;
- they come back and refer others.

### Illustrative example: two campaigns

| | Campaign A — "lowest price" | Campaign B — clear position |
|---|---|---|
| Ad spend | 10,000,000 so‘m | 10,000,000 so‘m |
| Cost per lead | 20,000 so‘m | 50,000 so‘m |
| Leads | 500 | 200 |
| Lead to sale | 4% | 15% |
| Sales | 20 | 30 |
| Average order | 600,000 so‘m | 1,200,000 so‘m |
| Revenue | 12,000,000 so‘m | 36,000,000 so‘m |

(Illustrative example.) On cost per lead, A wins. On revenue, B is three times ahead. That's why the key metric isn't cost per lead but **revenue per so‘m spent**.

## How to define your position: 4 steps

1. **Look at your best customers.** Who pays the most, haggles the least and comes back? Who are they, and why did they choose you? — [target audience and customer persona](/en/blog/target-audience-and-customer-persona/)
2. **Write down what competitors say.** Pick out the words that repeat in their ads — you need to say something different — [competitor analysis](/en/blog/competitor-analysis/).
3. **Choose one clear promise.** Not "everything", but the one thing that matters most to your best customers: timing, guarantee, result, service format — [unique selling proposition](/en/blog/unique-selling-proposition/).
4. **Add proof.** A case with numbers, a "before → after", a customer review. A promise without proof is just another "quality service".

## What to change in your ads

- **Say who you're talking to in the first line.** "If you're a [segment]…" — the right person stops, the wrong one scrolls on.
- **Show the price or a price range.** It cuts random leads.
- **Add 1–2 qualifying questions to the form.** For example, "When do you need it?" or "Approximate budget?". On formats — [lead form or messages](/en/blog/lead-form-or-messages/).
- **Judge campaigns by sales.** Track in the CRM which campaign's leads turn into sales.

## How to judge: 3 metrics

| Metric | Formula | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Cost per sale | Ad spend ÷ sales | What a real customer costs |
| Revenue per lead | Revenue ÷ leads | How much money one lead brings on average |
| LTV | Lifetime profit per customer | Accounts for returns and referrals |

More — [customer acquisition cost (CAC)](/en/blog/customer-acquisition-cost-cac/), [repeat sales and LTV](/en/blog/repeat-sales-and-ltv/).

## When a cheap lead really is good

A cheap lead isn't bad if it turns into a sale. Cheap leads work well:

- for low-ticket products with quick decisions;
- when the sales team is strong and qualifies leads fast;
- when the campaign is optimised for sales and lead-to-sale conversion is stable.

The problem isn't the cheap lead — it's making it the only goal.

## Common mistakes

- Judging an ads specialist or agency only by cost per lead.
- Inflating lead numbers with "free" offers and big discounts.
- Repeating the words competitors use in their ads.
- Hiding the price and spending time explaining it on every lead.
- Changing position while still running the old "lowest price" campaigns.

## Questions and answers

### If leads get more expensive, isn't that a bad sign?

Not if cost per sale and revenue improve. Judge cost per lead together with sales and revenue, not on its own.

### Does a small business need positioning?

Especially so. In a race to the lowest price a small business loses to big chains, but it can win with a clear segment and quality of service.

### How soon do results show after repositioning?

Lead cost and quality usually shift within a few weeks. Revenue changes depend on the sales cycle — faster for short cycles, later for long ones.

### What if I'm no different from my competitors?

Then the strategy's first task is to create a difference: service format, guarantee, timing, specialisation or the way you work with customers.

## Conclusion

Cheap leads and flat revenue are often two sides of one problem: ads that say the same thing to everyone, so you compete on price. A clear position, proof and qualifying questions may make leads a bit pricier, but they raise revenue per so‘m spent.
