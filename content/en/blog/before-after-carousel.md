A before–after carousel is the clearest post a service business can publish: people see what changed at a glance. Yet it often fails, because the "before" was shot from one corner and the "after" from another, so the viewer sees two different rooms instead of a difference. Below are the shooting rules and a ready 5-slide structure for renovation, furniture and cleaning services.

![Why a before–after carousel works](/img/blog/illus/oldin-keyin-karuseli-1.jpg 1200x675)

## Why a before–after carousel works

You cannot try a service in advance. A sofa can be tested in the showroom; a renovation cannot. So the customer looks for two answers: "have you handled a case like mine" and "how did it turn out".

A carousel answers both in order. The first slide is a familiar problem, the next is the result, then comes how it was done. People keep swiping because they want to see the ending.

A carousel also has an edge over video: you can stop and look closely. A seam, a corner, the grout between tiles — quality shows in small places. If you want to show the process itself, a [timelapse video](/en/blog/timelapse-video-for-shops/) is more convenient; for the result, a carousel is enough.

The format suits:

- renovation and finishing work, bathrooms, kitchens;
- custom furniture and reupholstery;
- home and office cleaning, carpet and upholstery cleaning;
- facades, signage, yards and landscaping;
- car interior cleaning and polishing.

## Shooting from one spot: 6 rules

The whole format rests on one idea: between the two photos, only the result should change. Everything else — angle, height, light — stays the same.

1. **Mark the spot.** Remember where you took the "before": the door frame, the windowsill, a piece of masking tape on the floor. If the job lasts two weeks, do not rely on memory.
2. **Keep the same height.** The easiest way is to always hold the phone at chest level or set the tripod to the same height.
3. **Use the same lens.** Shoot "before" with the main camera and "after" with the ultra-wide, and the room will look bigger. That misleads the customer and hurts you: a person who visits the site will notice.
4. **Light and time of day.** If you can, take both photos at the same time of day. A "before" under a yellow evening lamp and an "after" in midday sun means half the difference belongs to the light.
5. **Go vertical.** A vertical photo takes up more of the feed. Leave some room at the edges for cropping later.
6. **Take the "before" before work starts.** The most common problem is that there is no "before" photo at all. Make it a required task on the crew's first day.

Turn on the camera grid before shooting. When the wall and floor lines sit in the same place in both photos, the eye sees only the change as the slides switch.

> Do not make the "before" look bad on purpose: dark, crooked, with rubbish in the frame. People sense it and stop trusting the "after" too.

## The 5-slide structure

A carousel can hold many slides, but five are enough for one project. Each slide has one job.

| Slide | What it shows | Job | Text |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The "before" photo or a split frame | Stop the scroll | Project and task: "Yunusabad, 12 m² kitchen" |
| 2 | The "after" from the same spot | Show the result | No text, or one word: "After" |
| 3 | 1–2 shots of the process | "How it was done" | What was done: 3 short points |
| 4 | Close-up: seam, corner, hardware | Prove the quality | One detail: a material or a method |
| 5 | Wide view of the result | Next step | Timeline, what is included, call to action |

A few practical notes.

**Should the first slide be "before" or "after"?** Both work. If the "before" state is familiar and painful (a worn-out bathroom, a stained sofa), start with it. If the result is striking, put a split frame first: "before" on the left, "after" on the right. Try both in turn and see which one gets saved more.

**The second slide needs no text.** The viewer has just seen the "before". If the spot is the same, the difference explains itself.

**The fourth slide is the most underrated.** A wide shot looks good, but a close-up builds trust. For a furniture maker it is the drawer runner and the edge banding, for a renovation crew the tile seam and the corner, for a cleaning service the texture of the fabric.

**Do you have to put a price on the fifth slide?** No, but answer "what does it depend on": area, material, timeline. We covered the pros and cons in [should you show price in ads](/en/blog/should-you-show-price-in-ads/).

## Slide text and caption: a sample

Slide text must be short — it is read on a phone in a second. Details belong in the caption.

A simple caption outline:

- **Task.** What the customer asked for: "refresh an old kitchen without moving the plumbing".
- **Constraint.** What was hard: a small area, a tight deadline, existing pipes.
- **Solution.** What was done, in 2–3 points.
- **Timeline.** How long the work took.
- **Call to action.** Make the next step clear: "Message us in Direct to book a measurement".

A sample for a furniture workshop (illustrative example):

> "The customer wanted a dishwasher to fit into a 9 m² kitchen. We re-planned the bottom row, added a rotating shelf to the corner cabinet and took the upper cabinets up to the ceiling. From measurement to installation: 4 weeks. To get your kitchen estimated, send a photo of the floor plan in Direct."

There is no self-praise here, yet the reader learns three things: you understand tricky tasks, how long it takes, and where to start.

## Customer consent and ad restrictions

Renovation and cleaning happen in someone else's home. Family photos, documents, children or the address can end up in the frame. So:

- ask for consent to shoot and publish before work starts — a clause in the contract is the easiest way;
- remove personal items from the frame and do not show house or car plate numbers;
- if the customer objects, use close-ups only or do not publish the project at all.

For cases where people appear in the frame, see our guide to [photo and video consent for advertising](/en/blog/photo-video-consent-for-advertising/).

One more boundary. This article is about objects and spaces: a room, furniture, a carpet, a facade. Before–after content about the human body and appearance (weight loss, skin, cosmetic procedures) is a different subject. Meta's advertising standards include a [separate policy on personal health and appearance](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/personal-health-and-appearance): an ad must not create a negative self-perception. If you are preparing a carousel for a clinic or a salon, read that page first.

## 7 common mistakes

1. **No "before" photo.** People remember it when the job is done. The fix is a required task on day one.
2. **Different angles.** The viewer sees two rooms, not a difference.
3. **Filters and heavy editing.** If the "after" has a filter, the customer takes the brighter colour for the result. Edit both photos the same way, and lightly.
4. **Too many slides.** Fifteen photos of one project are an album, not a carousel. Pick the best five.
5. **Small, long text on a slide.** Details go into the caption.
6. **No call to action.** The last slide ends on a nice photo and the viewer does not know what to do next.
7. **Only perfect projects.** Alongside large, expensive jobs, show ordinary small ones — that is what most customers' homes look like.

## Where else to use the carousel

One good pair of photos works in several places:

- **A dedicated Highlight.** A first-time visitor sees your work in one place. See [an Instagram profile that sells](/en/blog/instagram-profile-that-sells/) for how to set the profile up.
- **In ads.** A carousel that gets saved a lot is worth testing in paid campaigns.
- **In the sales team.** When someone asks "have you done something similar?", the manager sends a link to the finished carousel. It is the simplest form of [social proof](/en/blog/social-proof-in-ads/).
- **On map listings.** Add the "after" photos to your Google and Yandex Maps pages.

How many such posts a month? There is no fixed norm. A useful guide is one carousel per finished project. That way part of your [content plan](/en/blog/instagram-content-plan/) fills itself.

## Questions and answers

### What if there is no "before" photo left?

Ask the customer — they often photographed the room themselves before the measurement. If there is none, make a "process — result — detail" carousel from this project and make the "before" photo mandatory from the next one. Never use a photo of another project as the "before".

### Carousel or Reels?

They do different jobs. Reels tend to reach more new people, while a carousel is studied closely and saved. You can make both from one project: a carousel from the photos and a short video from the process.

### Should the slides carry a logo?

A small logo in the corner does no harm and reminds people of you if the photo is reposted elsewhere. Just do not let it cover the main part of the image.

### What if the customer does not want the photos published?

Respect that decision. Try asking for permission to use detail and material shots without a wide view of the room. If that is also a no, the project stays out of the portfolio.

### How do you measure the result?

Look at saves, shares and the questions that arrive in Direct after the post, not at likes. If the manager asks every new enquiry "which of our projects did you see?", within a month you will know which carousel brings leads.

## Conclusion

A before–after carousel does not need expensive gear — it needs order: a photo taken before work starts, a marked spot, the same light and a clear job for every slide. Five slides — problem, result, process, detail and call to action — are enough for most service businesses.
