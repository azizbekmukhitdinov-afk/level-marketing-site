Many business owners track how many new customers arrive each month but have no idea how many quietly left. The ad budget grows, the sales team is busy, yet the customer base does not grow: it is a leaking bucket. This article shows how to calculate churn rate, which signals warn you before a customer leaves and how to win lost customers back, with numbers.

## What churn is and who counts as a "lost customer"

**Churn** is the share of customers who stopped working with you during a period. In a subscription model (education centre, gym, SMM retainer, internet provider) it is clear: payments stopped, the customer left. In a business without subscriptions (shop, café, beauty salon, clinic) customers never say "I'm leaving", they simply do not come back. So first you need to write down a rule.

A practical rule: if a customer has not bought for **2–3 times longer** than their usual interval, count them as lost. If people usually visit a barbershop every 3 weeks, a customer who has not come for 9 weeks is at risk, and at 12 weeks they are gone. Take the interval from your own data: calculate the average gap between purchases in your CRM or till system.

## The core formulas

| Metric | Formula | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Churn rate | Customers lost in the period ÷ customers at the start × 100 | How many customers you lost |
| Retention rate | (Customers at the end − new customers) ÷ customers at the start × 100 | How many existing customers stayed |
| Revenue churn | Monthly payments of lost customers ÷ monthly revenue at the start × 100 | The loss in money |
| Average "lifetime" | 1 ÷ monthly churn | How many months a customer stays on average |

Important: new customers do not go into the denominator. Otherwise churn looks artificially low in a month with strong advertising.

## A worked example

Illustrative example: an education centre has 200 students at the start of the month. During the month 30 new students join, and 190 remain at the end.

- Lost: 200 + 30 − 190 = **40 students**.
- Churn rate: 40 ÷ 200 = **20%**.
- Retention: (190 − 30) ÷ 200 = **80%**.

Even a modest monthly churn becomes serious over a year. Illustrative example: with 8% monthly churn, about 37% of the starting base is left after a year (0.92 to the power of 12), so almost two thirds leave.

Now let's turn it into money. Illustrative example: a 400,000 soum monthly fee and a 50% gross margin.

| Monthly churn | Average lifetime | Margin per customer (LTV) |
|---|---|---|
| 10% | 10 months | 2,000,000 soums |
| 8% | 12.5 months | 2,500,000 soums |
| 5% | 20 months | 4,000,000 soums |

Cutting churn from 10% to 5% doubles the value of a customer. That means you can pay twice as much to acquire a customer and still stay profitable. ## Why overall churn misleads: cohorts

A single overall number hides where the problem is. New customers leave several times more often than old ones: they have not formed a habit yet and have not seen a result. So group customers **by the month they joined** (cohorts) and look at how many of each group remain after 1, 2 and 3 months.

| Cohort | After 1 month | After 2 months | After 3 months |
|---|---|---|---|
| September | 70% | 60% | 56% |
| October | 72% | 63% | — |
| November | 85% | — | — |

Illustrative example: in this table the biggest loss happens in the first month. So the problem is not price or competitors, it is the experience of the first weeks. If first-month retention rose to 85% in November, find out what changed and make it the standard. We describe how to build the first 30 days in our article on [customer onboarding](/en/blog/customer-onboarding/).

## Why customers leave

Split the reasons into two groups: those you can influence and those you cannot. Moving away, a child finishing school, a client's business closing down are natural churn. You cannot bring it to zero, only account for it. The rest can be managed.

| Reason | What it looks like | Root cause |
|---|---|---|
| Expectations not met | "I thought it would be different" | Ads or the salesperson over-promised |
| No first result | Interest fades in 2–4 weeks | No onboarding |
| Service failure | A complaint, then silence | No standards or control |
| Price not worth the value | The "too expensive" excuse | Value was never shown |
| The customer forgot | No dissatisfaction at all | No contact or reminders |
| A competitor's offer | A sudden exit | A weak relationship |

Most customers who leave "because of price" actually leave because they did not feel a result. So always call a customer who left and ask an open question: "What changed?" Log the answers by category; our article on [collecting customer feedback](/en/blog/collecting-customer-feedback/) explains the routine.

## 8 early warning signs

Customers rarely leave all at once. Their behaviour changes a few weeks before they go. Flag these signals in your CRM:

| Signal | Where you see it | Action |
|---|---|---|
| The gap between visits or purchases grows | Till, CRM | A personal reminder, ask why |
| Missed classes or sessions | Attendance | A manager's call within 48 hours |
| Slow replies to messages | Telegram, Direct | Write again with one short, specific question |
| A complaint or negative comment | Chat, reviews | Solve it at manager level |
| A late payment | Accounting | Find out whether it is money or value |
| An NPS score of 0–6 | Survey | A call within 24 hours |
| In B2B, the contact person changed | Correspondence | An introductory meeting with the new person |
| A request for a discount or a "pause" | Sales team | An exit conversation, an alternative package |

You can turn the signals into a simple score: each signal is 1 point, and at 2 points or more the customer goes on a "red" list and a task is created for a manager. You do not need a complex system; Google Sheets or any [CRM](/en/blog/crm-for-small-business/) will do. For how to ask the NPS question, read [what is NPS](/en/blog/what-is-nps/).

## Three layers of reducing churn

**1. Prevention.** The cheapest churn is the churn that never happens. In ads and in sales, promise only what you can deliver. In the first 2 weeks the customer should see a result, even a small one, as long as it is concrete. Write down your service standards and check them.

**2. Saving.** When a customer says "I'm stopping", do not agree straight away and do not pressure them either. An exit conversation has three questions: what changed, what would make you stay, which option works for you now. There is often an alternative: a cheaper package, a different schedule, a one-month freeze.

**3. Win-back.** A lost customer is not a cold lead. They know you, and you do not have to introduce yourself again through ads. But the win-back sequence needs structure.

## A win-back sequence for lost customers

| After leaving | Channel | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 7 days | Call | Learn the reason, no offer |
| 30 days | Telegram or SMS | What has changed; if the reason was fixed, say so |
| 60 days | Message | A specific offer to come back, with a deadline |
| 90 days | Retargeting | An audience from your base, news or a case study |

Two technical notes. First, a Telegram bot cannot message a user first: according to Telegram's documentation, the user must message the bot or add it to a group before it can write to them ([core.telegram.org/bots](https://core.telegram.org/bots)). So connect customers to your bot at the moment of purchase, not later. For message frequency, see [Telegram bot broadcasts: 7 rules](/en/blog/telegram-bot-broadcast-rules/). Second, when you use customers' phone numbers and data for marketing, follow Uzbekistan's Law "On Personal Data" No. ZRU-547, including its consent rules ([lex.uz](https://lex.uz/docs/4396419)).

Do not turn the win-back offer into a permanent discount. If customers learn that "leave and they'll give you a discount", you are rewarding churn. A better option is to show the reason was fixed or to add value: a free consultation, a bonus lesson, a free check-up.

## Common mistakes

- Not measuring churn at all and looking only at new leads.
- Adding new customers into the formula to make the number look good.
- Treating every lost customer the same, so natural and manageable churn get mixed up.
- Not asking why a customer left, or sending only a form.
- Trying to save customers with discounts alone.
- Having signals but no owner and no deadline for acting on them.

## Questions and answers

### What churn rate is normal?

There is no universal benchmark: it depends on the industry, the price and the contract type. Instead of comparing yourself with other businesses, watch your own trend. If churn falls quarter after quarter and first-month retention grows, you are on the right track.

### How often should churn be calculated?

In a subscription model, monthly. In a business without subscriptions, monthly too, but over a window that matches your purchase interval (for example, the last 90 days). Updating the cohort table once a quarter is enough.

### Does advertising affect churn?

Yes. Customers who came in on overblown promises leave faster because their expectations were inflated. Compare retention by channel: sometimes the channel with the cheapest lead brings the most expensive customer.

### Does a small business need to track churn?

A small business especially. Losing 10 out of 100 regular customers every month is a problem that costs more than finding new ones. Start with a simple table: customer, date of last purchase, status.

## Conclusion

Churn is not just a number; it is the tool that shows the most expensive leak in your business. First write down your "lost customer" rule, calculate the formula correctly, split it into cohorts and assign an owner to the early signals. Then make prevention, saving and win-back a system.
