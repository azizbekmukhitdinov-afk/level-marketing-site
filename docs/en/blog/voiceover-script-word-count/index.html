A script looks great on paper, then in the studio the voice talent can't fit it into 15 seconds: they either rush, or the call to action at the end gets cut. Before you write a voiceover script for a video ad, answer one question: how many words fit into this length at all? This article shows a practical way to count words, time the read with a stopwatch and cut what doesn't fit.

Why word count matters

A long script causes three problems:

  • The speaker rushes. People hear fast speech but don't remember it. Price, address and product name get swallowed first.
  • Captions can't keep up. Many people watch ads with the sound off, so the words need to be on screen too. A long line on screen is hard to read in time.
  • No room for the must-haves. The call to action, the logo, the disclaimer in a loan ad all need time. When the script is long, those are the first things to go.

We covered how to choose the length of an ad separately — video ad length: 6, 15, 30 or 60 seconds. This article is about the next step: the length is chosen, now the script has to fit it.

How many words fit in 15 and 30 seconds: a rough table

There is no single standard: every speaker has their own pace. The English voiceover industry often works with about 2.5 words per second, or 150 words per minute (Voices.com). Uzbek words carry suffixes and tend to be longer — «buyurtmangizni» ("your order") is one word but takes a while to say — and Russian words are longer than English ones too, so fewer of them fit into a second.

Treat these numbers as a hypothesis to check, not a rule. Your own speaker gives you the real number — the method in the next section takes about ten minutes.

Timing with a stopwatch: 5 steps

  1. Pick a sample text. A ready 40–60 word text in the brand's voice, such as an old ad or post.
  2. Have the speaker read it at a normal pace. Not a "commercial voice", but the way they will sound in the ad. Record it on a phone.
  3. Do three takes and average them. The first take is usually slower, the third faster.
  4. Work out words per second. Divide the word count by the seconds. For example (illustrative example): 48 words read in 28 seconds — 48 ÷ 28 ≈ 1.7 words per second.
  5. Set the budget. Multiply speaking time by that pace: 13 seconds × 1.7 ≈ 22 words. That is your ceiling for a 15-second ad.

Write this number into your script template. From then on, counting words takes a minute and you save time in the studio. Script structure itself — Reels scripts: 5 ready-made templates.

A timing sheet: splitting the script by seconds

Knowing the total is not enough — plan how long each part takes. Write the script as a table: time on the left, voice in the middle, picture and on-screen text on the right.

7 rules for cutting a script

If the script runs long, cut in this order:

  1. Drop greetings and intros. "Dear customers", "we are pleased to offer" eat time and add nothing.
  2. Keep one adjective out of two. Instead of "high-quality and reliable", one precise word. Or a fact instead of an adjective.
  3. Put numbers on screen. "1,290,000 som" is one number on paper, but read aloud in Uzbek it becomes seven words. Show the price as text and let the speaker say "price on screen", or skip it.
  4. Don't read out phone numbers and addresses. Nobody remembers them by ear. Put them on screen and give one action in the voice: "Message us on Direct".
  5. Don't narrate what the picture shows. The voice should add to the shot, not repeat it: the shot shows a cabinet, the voice says why it's convenient.
  6. One ad, one idea. Three benefits make three ads. Three ideas in 15 seconds leave room for none.
  7. Never cut the call to action. Cut from the middle. The last 2 seconds belong to the call to action.

One ad in two languages: Uzbek and Russian versions

Many businesses in Uzbekistan run the same ad in two languages. A common mistake is translating the script word for word and laying it over the same timing. Translated text often comes out longer, and the speaker rushes again.

The right way:

  • set a separate word budget for each language (each has its own speaker and pace);
  • adapt rather than translate: the same idea, in a short, natural line for that language;
  • read both versions over the same shots and check with a stopwatch;
  • if one language doesn't fit, cut the words, not the shots.

Time for mandatory text and the call to action

In some ads part of the text is mandatory. For example, in ads for loans and instalment plans the disclaimer takes a set share of the ad's time. In that case, calculate the word budget from the time that's left: place the mandatory part and the call to action first, then see what remains for the main script. Finally, watch the ad once with the sound off. Can you read the captions in time? If not, the script is still too long.

Questions and answers

How many words does a 15-second ad need?

An illustrative guide is 20–25 words in Uzbek and 25–30 in Russian. But time your own speaker with a stopwatch to get the real number: it's ten minutes of work.

Can I ask the speaker to read faster?

You can, but it rarely helps: people hear fast speech but don't remember it. Cutting the script almost always works better.

What about people who watch with the sound off?

Add captions and put the key points (offer, price, call to action) on screen in large text. The ad should make sense without sound.

Does the maths change with an AI voice?

You can adjust the speed, but the rules stay the same: check stress and pauses in Uzbek and time the final file with a stopwatch. More — AI voice-over and dubbing in ads.

Conclusion

A voiceover script starts with timing: first measure how many words per second your speaker says, then split the word budget across the parts of the ad and cut the excess by the rules. Price, phone and address go on screen; the last 2 seconds go to the call to action.
