What is merchandising: shelves, store zones and the checkout

What is merchandising? In short, it is placing products inside a store so that shoppers notice them, understand them and pick them up. Advertising brings people through the door; merchandising decides what they buy once they are inside. This guide covers practical rules for shelves, store zones, POS materials and the checkout area — for small shops, pharmacies and convenience stores in Uzbekistan.

What merchandising is and what it affects

Merchandising is the "silent salesperson" inside a store. It controls three things:

  • which products are seen — which shelf, which row;
  • how the shopper moves — the route from the entrance to the till;
  • how easy the decision is — price, category, a helpful note on the label.

The street-facing window is a separate part of the job: it pulls passers-by inside. Window rules are covered in our article on shop window displays; here we deal only with what happens past the door.

Two parties do merchandising. The store owner organises their own floor. A manufacturer or distributor hires merchandisers so that their product gets a good spot on other people's shelves. The rule is the same for both: if it is convenient for the shopper, it sells.

Store zones: from the entrance to the till

Look at your store as four zones, each with its own job.

ZoneShopper's stateWhat goes hereWhat doesn't
Entrance (first 2–3 metres)Eyes still adjusting, walking fastOpen space, baskets, signageSmall items, a pile of promo tags
Main aisleSearching and choosingNew arrivals, seasonal offers, bundlesStands that block the way
Demand zone (back wall)Coming for something specificDaily staples: bread, milk, water, common medicinesSlow-moving expensive items
CheckoutWaiting, glancing aroundSmall, cheap, self-explanatory itemsAnything that needs a long think

Retail researcher Paco Underhill, in his book "Why We Buy", called the entrance the "decompression zone": after walking in, people take a few steps to adjust and barely notice what is placed there. So put your main offer a little further inside, not right at the door.

The demand zone pulls shoppers through the whole store. Many convenience stores put bread and milk on the back wall: people come for them and see everything else on the way. But don't overdo it — in a small neighbourhood shop, deliberately sending people on a detour is annoying. Where customers pop in for a quick purchase, convenience matters more.

The golden shelf and shelf rules

Not every shelf sells equally. A unit is usually split into four levels:

  1. Top shelf — visible but awkward to reach. Back stock, large packs, brand blocks.
  2. The golden shelf — eye and hand level. Many guides put it at roughly 120–160 cm from the floor for adults. This is where your highest-margin product, or the one you want to push, goes.
  3. Hand level (up to the waist) — easy to see and grab. Popular, fast-moving items.
  4. Bottom shelf — heavy, cheap or bulky goods: water, flour, big packs of detergent.

For children's products the "golden shelf" sits lower, at a child's eye level. Be careful here: sweets placed within a child's reach can annoy parents.

Other shelf rules:

  • One product, one block. Don't scatter the same item around the store. A vertical block (one brand from top to bottom) stands out from a distance.
  • Face forward. Labels and names should face the shopper. Obvious, yet in many shops packs stand sideways.
  • No gaps. An empty shelf says "sold out, business is struggling". Restock on time.
  • Rotate by date. Items closer to expiry go to the front, new stock to the back. In groceries and pharmacies this is a must.
  • Price tag under every item. Show the price in som, in a readable size. Where people have to ask for prices, many simply walk past.

Illustrative example. A convenience store in Tashkent moved a higher-margin tea to the golden shelf and the popular budget tea to waist level. The example only shows the logic: the popular item stays within reach, the profitable one sits at eye level.

Planograms: planning the shelf on paper

A planogram is a diagram showing which product sits where on the shelf, and how many facings it gets. Chains draw them in special software; a small shop can use a simple table or a photo of the shelf with notes.

What a planogram gives you:

  • everyone stocks the shelf the same way, so order survives a change of staff;
  • you see quickly what has run out;
  • changes become measurable: "we moved the tea — how did sales change?"

How to build one:

  1. Pull one month of sales by category from your till report.
  2. Mark the best sellers and the highest-margin items.
  3. Put high-margin items on the golden shelf and fast movers at hand level.
  4. Photograph the shelf and pin the photo up for staff.
  5. After a month, compare category sales and adjust if needed.

When measuring, follow one rule: change one thing at a time. If you move the tea and run a discount at once, you won't know which one worked.

POS materials: useful and excessive

POS materials are point-of-sale advertising: wobblers, shelf strips, aisle stoppers, price cards, stands, displays. Manufacturers often hand them out for free, and the store hangs up everything. The shelf turns into a forest of flags.

How to choose:

MaterialWhen it helpsWhen it hurts
Shelf stripLabelling a category: "tea", "for kids"A different brand's strip on every shelf
Wobbler (card on a flexible stem)One new product or promotionFive wobblers on one unit
Aisle stopperWhere the shopper needs to pauseIn a narrow aisle — people bump into it
Freestanding displaySeasonal goods, bundles, a new brandWhen it blocks the aisle
Advice cardAnswering "which one should I pick?"When the text is tiny and long

A simple rule: one main message per field of view. If a shopper sees three promotions on one shelf, they remember none. When the window, POS materials and your Instagram promotion say the same thing, they reinforce each other.

Text on POS materials counts as advertising too: in the state language, prices in som, promotion dates and conditions stated clearly, as Uzbekistan's advertising law requires.

The checkout: a small area with a big payoff

The checkout area is the most valuable few square metres in a store. Shoppers stop and wait there anyway. Products that belong here share three traits:

  • cheap — bought without thinking;
  • small — carried out in one hand;
  • self-explanatory — no explanation needed: gum, batteries, water, wet wipes, a small gift.

The checkout is the simplest way to raise the average order. More on that in our article on upselling and cross-selling. To see the effect, track the number of items per receipt — how to calculate it is explained in the article on UPT.

Refresh the checkout area weekly. If the same item sits there for months, regular customers stop seeing it.

By store type: pharmacy, supermarket, clothing

Pharmacy

People usually come to a pharmacy with a specific goal and a question for the pharmacist. Merchandising here supports the consultation:

  • open shelves hold non-medicine categories: hygiene, cosmetics, vitamins, baby products;
  • category signs: "cold season", "for travel", "for your baby";
  • by the till: plasters, hygiene products, small items.

Open display and advertising of prescription medicines may be restricted — check the current requirements. How to grow pharmacy sales within the law is covered in our article on pharmacy marketing.

Supermarket and convenience store

The main tools are zones and category logic. Group products the way shoppers think: "breakfast" (bread, butter, jam, tea), "guests are coming" (sweets, fruit, drinks). Placing related items side by side leads to extra purchases.

Clothing store

Clothes sell as an outfit. A full look on the mannequin, and next to it each piece of that look in every size. Sizes always run in the same order: small to large, left to right. Shoppers shouldn't waste time hunting for theirs.

How to measure the result

Merchandising is done for sales, not for looks. Check it with simple metrics:

  • category sales — 2–4 weeks before and after a change;
  • average order value and items per receipt;
  • conversion — how many visitors bought something. Ways to count visitors are in a separate article;
  • out-of-stocks — how many times a week a shelf had gaps.

Seasons, holidays and promotions distort the picture, so compare with the same period last year where you can.

Common mistakes

  • Cramming the shelf so everything is visible. Nothing stands out.
  • A forest of POS materials. Every brand hangs its flag; shoppers read none.
  • No price, or one you can't read. People are shy to ask and leave.
  • A one-off planogram. A month later the shelf is chaos again.
  • Narrow aisles. Where a trolley or pram can't pass, nothing sells.
  • A checkout display unchanged for a year. Regulars no longer see it.

Checklist

  • The store is split into four zones, each with a clear job
  • The first metres inside the door are clear; the main offer sits a little deeper
  • High-margin items on the golden shelf, fast movers at hand level
  • Every item has a readable price in som
  • A planogram photo is pinned up for staff
  • One main POS message per field of view
  • The checkout holds cheap, small, self-explanatory items, refreshed weekly
  • Category sales, average order and conversion are compared monthly

Questions and answers

Are merchandising and window display the same thing?

No. The window brings people in from the street; merchandising shapes what and how much they buy inside. It helps when both carry the same idea, but their jobs differ.

Does a small shop need a merchandiser?

Usually not. It is enough for the owner or a senior salesperson to update the planogram monthly and tidy the shelves daily. Dedicated merchandisers matter more to manufacturers and distributors, who look after their products across many stores.

A manufacturer offers money for the golden shelf. Should I accept?

It is a common practice. Before agreeing, work out how much the product currently in that spot earns you and whether the new one will cover it. Put the terms in writing: duration, shelf position, POS materials.

How soon will I see results?

Changes at the checkout and on the golden shelf usually show within 2–4 weeks. A full floor re-layout is worth tracking for a month or two, because regular customers need time to get used to the new order.

Conclusion

Merchandising lifts sales without extra ad spend: split the floor into zones, put high-margin items on the golden shelf, make prices visible, cut back POS clutter and refresh the checkout regularly.
