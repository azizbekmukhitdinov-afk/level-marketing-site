When to turn off an ad: 6 decision rules for your campaigns

Opening the ad account every day and switching off whatever ad gave an expensive lead yesterday is a habit of many business owners and junior media buyers. The result: no ad ever finishes learning, the account lives in a permanent "test" state and the budget is spent without teaching you anything. Turning off an ad is a decision made by rules written in advance, not by mood. Here are six such rules and how to apply them.

Why switching off "by feel" hurts

Meta's algorithm goes through a learning phase for every new ad set. During that time results are unstable: one day a lead costs 30,000 soum, the next 90,000 (illustrative example). If you change or switch things off during that period, learning starts over. More in Meta learning limited.

The second reason is small numbers. Drawing conclusions from 3 leads is like flipping a coin. A single day's result is often random.

The third reason is that results show up with a delay. Today's lead becomes a sale next week. If you switch off ads looking only at the ad account, you may kill the ad that was bringing sales.

Before deciding: write down 3 numbers in advance

Before launching a campaign, write down:

  1. Target cost per sale — how much you're willing to pay for one customer. Calculate it from your unit economics.
  2. Target cost per lead — cost per sale × lead-to-sale rate. For example, you'll spend 500,000 soum per sale and conversion is 10%, so a lead up to 50,000 soum is fine (illustrative example).
  3. Test budget — how much you'll give an ad before judging it. A practical guide: 3–5 times the target cost per lead.

Without these numbers, every decision is made on mood.

6 decision rules

Rule 1: don't touch it until the test budget is spent

If the ad hasn't spent its test budget yet (for example, 3 × target cost per lead), don't switch it off — even if day one was bad. Exception: a technical error such as a wrong link, a broken form or a typo.

Rule 2: test budget spent, no leads — turn it off

You've spent 3–5 target lead costs and got zero leads. That's a signal: the creative or the offer isn't working. Turn it off and try a different idea instead of leaving the same creative "one more day".

Rule 3: leads come in, but at twice the target — change something

Don't switch off here; look for the cause: audience, creative or form. Change one thing at a time. If there's still no result after 1–2 iterations, turn it off.

Rule 4: cheap leads, no sales — check the CRM

The ad that looks best in the account sometimes delivers the worst leads. Check over two weeks how its leads convert into sales. If there are none, the "cheap" ad is actually the most expensive — see the cheap leads trap.

Rule 5: a good ad slowly declines — prepare a replacement

If an ad worked well for weeks and then cost per lead slowly rises along with frequency, that's creative fatigue. Don't kill it abruptly: put a new creative next to it and switch the old one off once the new one proves itself. More in creative fatigue.

Rule 6: better than target — leave it alone, scale carefully

Tweaking a well-performing ad to "make it even better" is a common mistake. If you raise the budget, do it in steps, not in a jump. More in before increasing your ad budget.

The rules in a table

SituationDecision
Test budget not spentWait (unless there's a technical error)
Test budget spent, no leadsTurn off, test a new idea
Cost per lead 2× targetChange one variable
Cheap leads, no salesCheck the CRM, turn off if needed
Gradual declineAdd a new creative, then replace
Better than targetLeave it, raise the budget step by step

Put this table where everyone who touches the ad account can see it.

When to review: a weekly rhythm, not a daily one

Looking at the account daily is fine — but only to catch technical issues: a rejected ad, an exhausted budget, a broken form. Decisions are made once a week, on a fixed day:

  1. For each ad: spend, leads, cost per lead.
  2. From the CRM: how many of last week's leads became sales or meetings.
  3. Decide by the table: wait, change or turn off.
  4. Write the decisions down — next week you'll check whether they worked.

The monthly version of this rhythm is described in the monthly marketing meeting.

Common mistakes

  • Switching off based on the first 24 hours.
  • Changing creative, audience and budget at the same time.
  • Looking only at cost per lead in the account and never checking sales.
  • Turning off a working ad without a replacement, leaving the account empty.
  • Not recording decisions: a month later nobody remembers what was switched off and why.

Questions and answers

How many days should I give an ad?

It's not about days but about money spent and data collected. On a small budget that's 7–10 days, on a larger one faster. The benchmark is spending the test budget.

Can I pause instead of turning off?

Yes, pausing an underperforming ad is the same as turning it off. But an ad switched back on after a long pause often restarts learning.

Cost per lead is on target, but sales say "the leads are bad" — who's right?

The numbers. Check in the CRM how many leads from that ad reached a sale or meeting over two weeks.

How many ads should a campaign have?

Three to five different creatives in one ad set is a good start. Too many ads split the budget so none of them collects enough data.

Conclusion

Turning off an ad is a decision made by rules written in advance. With a target cost per sale, a test budget and a weekly review, your account stops making random decisions and every soum spent teaches you something.
