On Instagram and Telegram you keep seeing offers like "earn from home", "start your own business", "join our team". Most of them are about network marketing, or MLM (multi-level marketing). Here is a marketer's look at how the model works, who makes money in it and how to tell an honest network company from a financial pyramid.

What MLM is, in plain words

Network marketing is a model where products are sold not through shops and advertising but through people. A participant (a "distributor", "partner" or "consultant") earns in two ways:

  1. From personal sales — selling products to friends and customers for a markup or commission.
  2. From the sales of people they recruit — bonuses from the lower levels of their "structure" or "team".

The second part is what separates MLM from ordinary retail, and it raises the most questions.

Who actually earns

Income in a network company is distributed like a pyramid: a few people at the top of the structure receive large bonuses, while most people at the lower levels earn little or lose money. The reason is simple maths:

  • to earn, each new participant has to recruit people themselves;
  • a circle of friends — even a whole city — is finite, so the market saturates quickly;
  • many companies require a minimum monthly purchase to keep your status.

As a result, many participants spend what they earn on products and training materials. So look past "I make $5,000 a month" success stories and ask for the company's official income disclosure — serious companies publish one.

The genuine upsides of MLM

To be fair, the model suits some people:

  • Almost no starting capital — no shop, warehouse or ad budget needed.
  • Flexible hours — it can be a side income alongside a main job.
  • Sales and communication skills — handling objections, presenting and staying in touch with customers are useful in any business later.

If you genuinely like the product and enjoy selling it to a clear circle of customers, MLM can be an extra source of income.

6 signs of a financial pyramid

Some "network companies" are really pyramids: the money comes from new members' fees, not from products. Financial pyramids are illegal in Uzbekistan, and taking part can bring legal trouble on top of lost money. Be careful if you see:

SignWhy it's dangerous
A large joining fee or "starter pack"Income comes from your payment, not the product
No real product, or one far pricier than the marketThe product is just a cover
The main promise is a bonus for recruitingThe model is built on recruitment, not sales
Guaranteed high incomeHonest businesses don't give guarantees
"Decide now, places are limited"Pressure is a sign of manipulation
No official company details, address or licenceIf money disappears, there's no one to claim from

Manipulation tactics

Some network structures keep people through psychological pressure. It helps to recognise the tactics:

  • Overwhelming attention and praise — newcomers are welcomed as "family", so voicing doubts feels awkward.
  • Only positive information — failures aren't discussed, and criticism is dismissed as "what lazy people say".
  • Guilt — "no results means you didn't try hard enough", "your family doesn't support you".

If you feel that kind of pressure, postpone the decision and look for independent information.

7 questions to ask before joining

  1. Where is the company registered, and does it have an official office in Uzbekistan?
  2. What share of income comes from product sales, and what share from recruiting?
  3. Is there a joining fee or mandatory monthly purchases?
  4. How does the product price compare with similar products in shops?
  5. Is there an official report on participants' average income?
  6. Will they take back unsold products if you leave?
  7. Would you buy this product if it weren't part of an MLM?

If two or three questions don't get a clear answer, that is a serious warning.

A marketer's view: what about building your own business?

What draws many people to MLM is not the product but the idea of owning a business. Today small businesses in Uzbekistan have direct sales channels: Instagram, Telegram, Uzum Market. When you sell your own product or service, you keep all the income, and the customer base and brand are yours. To get started, see our Instagram ads setup guide and our guide to ad costs.

How to calculate MLM income

Start from net income, not the promised bonus:

Net income = bonuses and markup − (mandatory purchases + events and training + travel and phone costs + advertising)

An example (illustrative numbers): a monthly bonus of 1,500,000 sum, but a mandatory monthly order of 800,000, events at 300,000 and travel and phone costs of 200,000 sum. Net income: 200,000 sum. Add the hours spent, too: the hourly income often turns out far lower than in an ordinary job.

How to spot an MLM pitch on social media

  • Vague promises like "passive income" and "financial freedom".
  • Photos of expensive cars and travel, but little about the product.
  • "Comment '+' for details" — the company name stays hidden until the very end.
  • Screenshots of earnings without context.
  • The focus is on "joining the team", not on the product.

If you've already joined

  1. Calculate your net income for the last three months using the formula above.
  2. Stop buying stock you can't sell.
  3. Check the contract's terms for returning unsold products.
  4. Don't pressure friends and relatives — relationships are worth more than income.
  5. If you see signs of a pyramid, decide on leaving.

The law and protecting yourself

Financial pyramid schemes are banned by law in many countries. If you come across a suspicious offer or can't get your money back, contact an independent lawyer or a consumer protection organisation. Never transfer money without documents.

What an honest referral programme looks like

If you want to grow your own business's sales through customers, you don't need MLM. An honest referral programme has no entry fee, pays rewards only for real purchases, has open and clear terms, and sells a product worth its price. A programme like that turns happy customers into your best advertising — see Repeat sales and LTV.

How to protect people close to you

If someone close to you gets drawn into MLM, don't blame or mock them — it only pushes them further away. Offer to calculate the net income together, ask for the company's documents and don't lend money for a "starter pack". Give them time to decide: decisions made under pressure are often wrong.

A simple test before deciding

  1. Would I buy this product at this price if I weren't a participant?
  2. Can I earn money from sales to customers alone?
  3. Is there an entry fee or a mandatory monthly purchase?
  4. Can unsold products be returned?
  5. Will my net income be positive after three months?

If you don't like several of your answers, think again before joining.

Questions and answers

Are all MLM companies pyramid schemes?

No. The key difference is where the income comes from: sales of a real product to outside customers, or payments from new participants. The latter is a sign of a pyramid.

A friend is inviting you to join — how do you say no?

Politely and briefly: "Thanks, but this isn't right for me now." There's no need to argue or try to convince them — keep the friendship.

If you like the product, do you have to join?

No. In many cases you can buy the product as an ordinary customer. Before joining, calculate the economics with the formula above.

Can you get back money paid into an MLM?

It depends on the contract and the company's rules. Get the refund terms in writing, keep your documents and consult a lawyer if needed.

Can you advertise MLM on social media?

Meta's advertising policies restrict ads for business models that promise quick income for little investment, including multi-level marketing opportunities. Such ads may be rejected, and the ad account restricted.

Will MLM experience be useful later?

The sales and communication skills can be. But you can also learn them in an ordinary sales job or in your own business — without the financial risk.

Can an online course or "mentorship" be a pyramid too?

Yes, if the income comes mainly from recruiting new students who then resell the course to others. The signs: little information about the course itself, a main promise of "you'll earn by bringing in others", and pressure to decide quickly.

Where can you find information about a company?

Check its official registration, legal address and website, and read former participants' reviews and the news. Ask for all terms in writing — verbal promises guarantee nothing.

Conclusion

Network marketing is a legal model, but stable income in it goes to a few. Know the signs of a pyramid, ask for numbers and don't decide on emotion. If your goal is your own business, Level Marketing can help build the strategy, content and advertising for your product.
