Commercial proposal template: a structure that sells

A commercial proposal often looks like this: three pages about the company, a list of services and a price at the end. The client opens it, looks at the price and never replies. The reason is simple: a proposal like that is about the company, not the client. Here's how a proposal that sells is structured, plus a ready-made template.

Cold vs warm proposals

Cold proposalWarm proposal
To whomSomeone you haven't spoken to yetAfter a meeting or call
GoalSpark interest and start a conversationHelp them make a decision
LengthShort: one pageDetailed: tailored to the client's task
PriceOften a "from" price or noneSpecific options

The insight: the best proposal is a warm one, sent after a conversation. Instead of firing off cold proposals, start the conversation first with a short message.

The 7 blocks of a proposal that sells

1. A headline about the client's result

Not "Level Marketing commercial proposal" → "X leads a month: an advertising plan for [Company]" (illustrative example).

2. The client's task, in their words

What you heard in the meeting: "You said leads are scarce and expensive, and your sales team isn't busy." It gives the client the feeling of being understood.

3. The solution

What you'll do, step by step. Not a list but a rationale: why these exact steps solve the client's problem.

4. The result and how it's measured

Which metrics, when, and what kind of report. Don't make promises — set out the agreed metrics and how they'll be measured — what is KPI.

5. Proof

A case from the same industry, a review, numbers (real ones only) — social proof.

6. Pricing with options

Instead of one price, offer 2–3 options: "Basic", "Optimal", "Extended". The client thinks "which one?" rather than "yes or no?". Spell out what's included in each option.

7. The next step and a deadline

"I'll call tomorrow at 15:00 to sign the contract" or "This offer is valid for 10 days". Without a concrete step, the proposal ends with "I'll think about it".

A template (1–2 pages)

  • [Client result]: a proposal for [Company]
  • The task. [2–3 problems the client named in the meeting]
  • The solution. 1) … 2) … 3) …
  • How we measure results. [Metrics, reporting frequency]
  • Why us. [1–2 cases or proof points]
  • Options. A — … / B — … / C — … [what's included + price]
  • Next step. [Date, time, who does what]

Choosing a format

  • PDF — the classic, for printing and forwarding.
  • A presentation — to show in a meeting — AI presentations.
  • An interactive map or page — to walk through a complex service step by step.

Whatever the format, the content is the same: the client's task, the solution, the result, the price, the next step.

After sending

  • It's often better to go through the proposal together in a meeting or call rather than just emailing it.
  • Call within 1–2 days: "Any questions?"
  • Be ready for "I'll think about it" — the "I'll think about it" follow-up.

Common mistakes

  • Ten pages, starting with the company's history.
  • A generic template with only the client's name changed.
  • Jargon the client doesn't understand.
  • A single price — no choice.
  • No next step.
  • Inflated numbers in promises.

Questions and answers

How long should a proposal be?

A cold one: one page. A warm one: 2–5 pages, depending on the client's task.

Should the price be in the proposal?

In a warm proposal, yes, with options. Hiding the price often reduces trust.

Can I write proposals with AI?

For a draft, yes — but the client's task and the numbers must come from you — AI sales scripts.

Conclusion

A commercial proposal that sells is about the client, not the company: their task, the solution, how results are measured, proof, pricing options and a concrete next step.
