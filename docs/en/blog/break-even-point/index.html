At the end of the month there is money in the till, but is there profit? Many owners can only guess. The break-even point gives a precise answer: how much you need to sell each month to cover all costs, after which every extra sale brings profit. In this article we calculate it in units and in money, show how the ad budget moves it, and walk through the whole calculation step by step for a coffee shop.

What the break-even point is

The break-even point is the sales volume at which revenue equals all costs. At that point profit is zero: no loss, no profit. Below it you lose money, above it you make money.

It is calculated in two forms:

  • in units: how many products, receipts, customers or orders you need;
  • in money: how much monthly revenue you need.

The first is handy for a sales plan, the second for a business with a wide range of products.

Fixed and variable costs

The whole calculation depends on splitting costs into two groups correctly.

CostTypeNote
RentFixedPaid regardless of sales
Base salariesFixedSales staff base pay goes here too
Sales commission, bonusesVariableDepends on each sale
Cost of goods or ingredientsVariablePer unit
Delivery, packaging, card feesVariablePer order
Utilities, internet, softwareMostly fixedMay have a small variable part
AdvertisingDepends on your approachAs a monthly budget, fixed; as a cost per order, variable

The key idea is contribution margin: what is left from the price after variable costs. Every sale covers fixed costs with this amount. How it differs from margin is shown in a table in margin vs markup.

The margin of safety is often forgotten, yet it is very useful: it shows by what percentage sales can drop before the business goes into the red.

Illustrative example: a coffee shop

The numbers are illustrative and only show the order of the calculation.

Step 1. Contribution per receipt. The average receipt is 50,000 soums. Ingredients for drinks and desserts, cup, lid and card fee come to 20,000 soums. Contribution is 30,000 soums, or 60% of the price.

Step 2. Monthly fixed costs.

ItemSoums
Rent15,000,000
Base salaries24,000,000
Utilities and internet3,000,000
Accounting, software, other3,000,000
Ad budget6,000,000
Total51,000,000

Step 3. Break-even point.

  • In units: 51,000,000 ÷ 30,000 = 1,700 receipts a month, about 57 a day (30 days).
  • In money: 51,000,000 ÷ 0.6 = 85,000,000 soums of monthly revenue.

Step 4. Target profit. The owner wants 15,000,000 soums of profit a month: (51,000,000 + 15,000,000) ÷ 30,000 = 2,200 receipts, about 73 a day.

Step 5. Margin of safety. The shop currently sells 2,000 receipts a month. (2,000 − 1,700) ÷ 2,000 = 15%. If sales fall by more than 15%, the coffee shop runs at a loss. For a rainy month or a newly opened location that is not much of a cushion.

How the ad budget moves the break-even point

If you treat advertising as a monthly budget and add it to fixed costs, every extra soum pushes the break-even point up. In the coffee shop example, fixed costs without ads are 45,000,000 soums.

A simple rule follows: every 3,000,000 soums of advertising must bring at least 100 extra receipts. In other words, one receipt from ads must not cost more than 30,000 soums, which is the contribution per receipt. If it costs more, the ads do not pay for themselves on the first purchase.

But a coffee shop customer does not come only once. If a new customer from ads returns on average 3 times a month, you can spend up to 90,000 soums to acquire them. Two conditions apply: repeat visits must be measured, not assumed, and there must be a system that brings customers back, see repeat sales and LTV. Work out what one new customer costs with CAC, and whether advertising pays off overall with ROMI.

A practical decision rule:

  • Maximum customer cost = contribution per sale × number of purchases in the period.
  • Actual customer cost is lower: you can raise the budget step by step.
  • Higher: fix the offer, conversion or average order first. Raising the budget only multiplies the loss, see before increasing your ad budget.

A business with many products

A shop or restaurant has hundreds of items, each with its own margin. Calculating in units is hard here, calculating in money is easier.

  1. Take total revenue and total variable costs for the last month.
  2. Contribution margin ratio = (revenue − variable costs) ÷ revenue.
  3. Break-even revenue = fixed costs ÷ that ratio.

Be careful: if the sales mix changes, so does the ratio. If customers shift to cheap, low-margin items, the break-even point rises even when revenue stays the same. So watch which products you put on promotion.

Turn the number into a daily plan

1,700 receipts a month is an abstract number for a team. Turn it into daily and weekly targets: 57 receipts a day is break-even, 73 is target profit. It is enough to compare the till or CRM report with these two lines every evening. If you see the shop falling behind mid-week, you can act without waiting for the month to end: change opening hours, the product range or the ad audience rather than prices.

Common mistakes

  • Treating revenue as profit. Money in the till is not profit yet.
  • Leaving out the owner's own pay. If the owner works in the coffee shop, their labour is a fixed cost too.
  • Excluding ads "for later". The ad budget moves the break-even point noticeably.
  • Forgetting seasonality. If you calculate with yearly averages, the low-season loss stays hidden.
  • Calculating once and forgetting. Rent, prices and ingredients change, and the point moves with them.

Questions and answers

How often should I recalculate the break-even point?

Once a month, together with your monthly report. Also before any big change: a new branch, a price change, higher rent or doubling the ad budget.

Where do loan payments go?

Interest is a fixed cost. Repaying the principal is not an expense in the profit calculation, but the cash still leaves the till. So keep two points: one for profit and one for cash flow. For the second, add the principal repayment to fixed costs too.

Is the break-even point the same as the payback period?

No. The break-even point is the volume that covers the current month's costs. The payback period is when your initial investment (renovation, equipment) comes back. The second clock only starts once you reach the first.

What is a "unit" in a service business?

Your most common unit of sale: a customer, a membership, a session or a project. If service prices vary a lot, use the money formula instead.

Conclusion

The break-even point is one of the simplest and most useful numbers in a business. Once you know it, your sales plan, maximum customer cost and ad budget limit all come out of one table. Split fixed and variable costs, recalculate monthly, and know in advance how many extra sales each soum of advertising has to bring.
