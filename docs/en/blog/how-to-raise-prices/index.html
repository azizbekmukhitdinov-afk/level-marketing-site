How to raise prices without losing customers: 6 steps

Raw materials got more expensive, rent went up, wages rose — but prices haven't changed in a year. Many business owners are afraid to raise prices: "customers will leave", "competitors are cheaper". Meanwhile, profit quietly shrinks every month. Raising prices is an inevitable and normal process for any business. What matters is doing it right. Here are six steps to raise prices without losing customers.

Signs it's time

  • Costs have risen and profit has fallen — the numbers show it.
  • Demand exceeds supply: queues, waiting lists, full capacity.
  • Customers are surprised: "that's cheap".
  • Competitors have already raised prices.
  • Quality or service has improved, but the price hasn't.

If two or three of these apply, it's time to review your prices.

Step 1: do the maths first

Some customers may leave when prices rise. The question is how many can leave before profit drops. Illustrative example:

NowAfter a 10% increase
Price1,000,000 soum1,100,000 soum
Cost of goods and expenses700,000 soum700,000 soum
Profit per sale300,000 soum400,000 soum
Sales per month100?
Monthly profit30,000,000 soum75 sales are enough for 30,000,000 soum

In this example, a 10% price rise leaves profit unchanged even if sales fall by 25%. In practice, a much smaller share of customers usually leaves. This calculation turns fear into a number. How to calculate — unit economics.

Step 2: choose the method

MethodHowWhen it fits
All at onceAll prices change on one dayA small increase, few products
In stagesNew customers first, loyal ones laterMany loyal customers
Through tiersA new premium tier; the old one with less valueServices, memberships
New collection / versionNew product, new priceManufacturing, clothing, furniture
With added valueThe price rises, but something is addedStrong competition

Working with tiers — entry offer.

Step 3: warn loyal customers in advance

If a loyal customer discovers the increase at the till, it stings. Warning them in advance shows respect:

  • timing: usually 2–4 weeks ahead;
  • channel: a personal message, Telegram, a call from their salesperson;
  • an option: "you can order at the old price until 1 December" — often this also creates a short wave of sales.

Step 4: explain the reason honestly

Customers accept a price increase when the reason is clear. A good explanation is:

  • short and honest: "raw materials went up", "we bought new equipment";
  • clear about what isn't changing: quality, warranty, service;
  • showing anything that has improved.

The most powerful format is a short video from the owner. More in founder on camera.

What not to do: send a long letter full of apologies — it makes the increase look like "bad news".

Step 5: prepare your sales team

Salespeople meet the new price first. If they're unprepared, they say "yes, it's gone up, sorry" and try to keep the customer with a discount. Prepare them with:

  • the new price and the reason, in a short text;
  • an answer to the "it's too expensive now" objection;
  • when the old price can still apply (for example, prepaid orders).

Answering price questions — replying to "How much?".

Step 6: measure the result

Track for 1–2 months after the increase:

  • number and value of sales;
  • average order value;
  • profit;
  • the share of new vs loyal customers;
  • lost reasons in the CRM: how much did "too expensive" grow.

If sales drop more than expected, find out why: the price itself, or not enough explanation.

Marketing during a price increase

Your ads should change too:

  • creatives focused on value, not price — more in premium segment marketing;
  • update any creatives that show the old price;
  • content answering objections: "why it costs this much" — content from customer objections.

And don't try to "offset" the increase with a discount — that takes you straight back to the old price. More in the discount trap.

Common mistakes

  • Waiting several years and then raising sharply — more painful than small regular increases.
  • Not warning loyal customers.
  • Deciding out of fear, without the maths.
  • Not preparing the sales team.
  • "Compensating" with a discount right after the increase.

Questions and answers

How much can I raise prices?

It depends on costs, competition and value. Start with the maths: how many customers could leave before profit falls.

What if competitors don't raise their prices?

If the difference between you and competitors is clear — service, quality, speed — a price gap is justified. If there's no difference, create one first. More in USP.

Should I announce the increase or raise prices quietly?

Always tell loyal customers. New customers don't need an announcement — they only see the new price.

How often can prices go up?

For many businesses, once a year is a regular, expected practice. With high inflation, it may be more often.

Conclusion

Raising prices is maths, a plan and communication. Calculate profit first, choose a method, tell loyal customers early and honestly, prepare your team and measure the result. During price changes, Level Marketing adapts ads and content so customers see value, not just the price.
