Store foot traffic: how to count visitors and conversion

Most shop owners know yesterday's takings by heart but have no idea how many people walked through the door. So when sales drop, the cause stays unclear: did fewer people come, or did the people who came not buy? Counting store foot traffic answers that question, and it does not take expensive equipment. Below are three methods, a simple table and a way to read the numbers.

What counting store foot traffic gives you

A shop's sales are made of three numbers: how many people came in, what share of them bought, and how much they spent on average. We covered that formula in detail in our article on how to increase sales in a store. The till gives you the last two. Nobody writes down the first.

Without a visitor count, two different problems look the same:

SituationVisitsConversionWhere the problem is
Sales felldownunchangedAdvertising, signage, shop window, location
Sales fellunchangeddownSales staff, range, price, queues
Sales did not growupdownThe shop floor cannot handle the flow
Sales grewunchangedupWork on the shop floor improved

In the first case the money belongs in advertising, in the second in training your staff. Without counting you cannot tell them apart, which is why money often goes to the wrong place.

Who counts as a visit: 5 rules

Write the rule down once before you start. If it changes along the way, you cannot compare one week with another.

  1. Staff do not count. Sales assistants, security, cleaners, couriers and suppliers are not visits.
  2. People who arrive together are one visit. A couple or a mother and daughter usually pay with one receipt. Count them as two and your conversion comes out artificially low. Treat a family or a group of friends as one "buying unit".
  3. Children with adults are not counted separately. Even in a children's shop, adults make the decision.
  4. Someone who steps back in is not a new visit. If a person goes out to take a call and returns a minute later, do not mark them twice.
  5. Count only those who enter. People who stop at the window and walk on are a different metric. You can note it separately to judge how your shop window performs.

The second rule matters most. An automatic counter does not recognise groups and counts every head. That is why conversion from a manual count and from a counter will not match, so keep them in separate tables.

Three methods: by hand, from receipts, with a counter

By hand: a sheet of paper or a tally clickerclose to zero, but it takes staff timegood enough if the rule is followed; more errors at peak hoursA small shop with up to 300 visits a day, a first measurement

Counting by hand is the right way to start. It is cheap and it handles groups correctly. The one weakness: when the assistant is busy with a customer, the count gets forgotten.

The number of receipts shows purchases, not visits. You still need it to work out conversion, so pull receipts by hour from the till report every day. Leave out refund receipts.

Counters come in three kinds: an infrared beam at the door, a ceiling sensor, and software running on a CCTV camera you already have. An infrared beam is cheap, but it can register two people walking in side by side as one. A ceiling sensor is more accurate and costs more. Manufacturers usually quote accuracy of around 95%, yet industry commentary notes this is often a lab result; in practice accuracy depends on the shape of the entrance, ceiling height and displays near the door (Teledyne overview). So even after installation, stand next to it for a few hours and compare it with a manual count.

Do not rush to buy hardware. Count by hand for two weeks first. That will show whether you need these numbers and whether you actually make decisions from them.

Counting by hand: a two-week routine

  1. Prepare a sheet. Columns are days of the week, rows are opening hours (10–11, 11–12 and so on). Each visit is one tally mark. The sheet stays by the till.
  2. Name one person responsible. "Everyone counts" means nobody does. One person per shift, for example the cashier or the assistant standing closest to the door.
  3. Write the rule at the top of the sheet. The same five points, in short form.
  4. In a busy shop, sample. Count the first 15 minutes of every hour and multiply by 4. It is an estimate, but good enough to compare days and hours. Keep the method the same for the whole period.
  5. Move the numbers into a spreadsheet each evening. A plain Google Sheets table: date, hour, visits, receipts, revenue.
  6. Count for at least two full weeks. One week can be chance: rain, payday, a promotion next door. What repeats over two weeks is a pattern.

If staff see the count as surveillance, the numbers will be distorted. Tell them plainly what it is for: the table is not there to punish anyone but to show which hours need an extra person on the floor.

Working out conversion: formula and example

The formula is simple:

Conversion = number of receipts ÷ number of visits × 100%

Calculate it by day of the week at least, and by hour if you can, not just for the day as a whole. We have a separate article on conversion rate in general; this one is only about the shop floor.

An illustrative example: a clothing shop in Tashkent, one week (the figures are invented to show the method):

DayVisitsReceiptsConversion
Monday1101715.5%
Tuesday1051615.2%
Wednesday1151815.7%
Thursday1301914.6%
Friday1602213.8%
Saturday2603111.9%

Total1,12015013.4%

The interesting part of this table is the weekend. Twice as many people come in, but conversion falls from 15% to 11–12%. On Saturday and Sunday 500 people entered and 58 receipts were issued. Had the weekday 15% held on those days, there would have been 75 receipts, a difference of 17 purchases a week. The cause is usually on the floor: too few assistants, a queue for the fitting room, a wait at the till. That is fixed with a shift schedule, not with advertising.

What counts as "good" conversion? Dor, a foreign maker of door counters, lists ranges of 15–25% for clothing shops, 10–20% for gifts and homeware, and 40–60% for grocery in its guide (Dor guide). These come from a vendor and from another market, so do not treat them as a norm. Comparing yourself with your own previous week is more useful.

What the numbers tell you and what to do

After two weeks, ask the table three questions.

In which hours are visits high and conversion low? Those are the hours of lost purchases. Put an extra assistant on, move the lunch break, open a second till.

On which days or hours are visits very low? Staff are idle then. Move stocktaking, content shoots and calls to your customer base into those hours.

How do visits change from week to week? Mark in the table the date you launched an ad, a new sign or festive decoration. Compare the two weeks before with the two weeks after, and you will see whether outdoor advertising and decoration pay for themselves. For a sharper measurement, add QR codes and promo codes.

In December this table is especially useful. Pre-holiday flow builds, and last week's hourly numbers become the basis for next week's shift schedule. And a December you counted this year becomes the one reliable reference when you plan the season next year.

Common mistakes

  • Counting a single day. One Saturday proves nothing. Two weeks at least.
  • Changing the rule midway. Count a group as one in week one and each person separately in week two, and conversion will "drop" although nothing changed.
  • Treating receipts as visits. More receipts does not mean more foot traffic; conversion may have risen instead.
  • Not checking the counter. Hardware can count staff, couriers and trolleys. Once a month, count by hand for an hour and compare.
  • Collecting numbers and changing nothing. If the table does not affect shift schedules, ad timing or how the floor is run, counting is wasted effort.

Questions and answers

Do online orders count towards conversion?

No. For shop-floor conversion, use only receipts issued in the shop. Keep orders that came through Instagram or Telegram and were collected in store on a separate line, otherwise floor conversion will look higher than it is.

How do you count in a small shop inside a mall?

The same way: mark a clear entry line, for example where the floor colour changes. A person who crosses it is a visit. The mall's figures for overall flow say nothing about traffic into your particular shop.

Can you count from a CCTV camera?

Yes. You can watch the recording at high speed and count by hand, or connect people-counting software to the camera. The first option works well for spot checks, for example two hours a week. Use the footage for counting only.

How long should you keep counting?

Two weeks is enough for a first picture. After that, repeat for a week every quarter and after each major change (a new window, advertising, opening hours). If the flow is heavy and the numbers already drive decisions, it is time to install a counter.

Conclusion

Counting store foot traffic is the cheapest way to find the cause when sales fall. One sheet, one responsible person and two weeks: after that the number shows whether you need advertising or need to fix what happens on the floor.
