Cashback marketing: mechanics, maths and how it beats discounts

Cashback marketing means returning part of a purchase to the customer as points and inviting them back for the next one. Set up well, cashback costs less than a discount and brings customers back. Set up badly, it becomes yet another discount that eats margin on every receipt. Here are the mechanics, the maths and the common mistakes.

What cashback is and when it works

The general types of loyalty schemes — points, tiers, stamp cards, members' clubs — are covered in our article on loyalty programmes. Here we go deeper into cashback only: which rules to set, what it costs and how to measure it.

The idea is simple: "buy today and next time will be cheaper". It works when three conditions are met:

  • purchases repeat — customers come several times a month, or at least once a quarter;
  • competitors are close by — prices and range are similar, so customers choose where to go;
  • there is margin — the business can afford the cost of cashback.

Good fits: cafes and coffee shops, grocery stores, pharmacies, beauty salons, car washes, pet shops, cosmetics, children's goods.

Where cashback does little:

  • rare purchases — furniture, property, large appliances. The next purchase is years away and the points are forgotten;
  • very thin margins — cashback takes most of the profit; non-cash perks (free delivery, no-queue service) work better;
  • one-off customers — a shop by the train station, a tourist spot.

Cashback vs discount: what's the difference

At first glance a 5% discount and 5% cashback look the same. In practice they work differently.

FeatureDiscountCashback
When the customer benefitsNow, on this receiptOn the next purchase
CostOn every receipt, in fullOnly when points are redeemed
Brings the customer backNoYes, points are a reason to return
Price perception"It's cheaper here" — the price dropsThe price stays the same
DataThe customer stays anonymousPhone number and purchase history

A permanent discount trains people to expect the lower price, and the regular price starts to feel "expensive". That risk is covered in detail in our article on the discount trap. Cashback leaves the price alone and gives you a customer base on top.

The maths: what cashback really costs you

Before choosing a rate, build one table. The figures below are an illustrative example, only to show the method.

Illustrative example. A cosmetics shop in Tashkent: average receipt 200,000 som, gross margin 35%, i.e. 70,000 som per receipt.

OptionCustomer getsReal cost to the shopMargin left per receipt
5% discount10,000 som immediately10,000 som60,000 som
5% cashback, all points redeemed10,000 points6,500 som (at cost of goods)63,500 som
5% cashback, 60% of points redeemed (illustrative)10,000 pointsabout 3,900 somabout 66,100 som

Why cashback is cheaper:

  1. Points are paid back in goods. When a customer redeems 10,000 points, you hand over 10,000 som worth of goods, but those goods cost you less — 6,500 som in the example.
  2. Not all points get used. Some expire or are forgotten. You can't predict this share in advance — measure it yourself over the first three months.
  3. To use points, the customer comes back. And often spends more than the points are worth.

But be honest in your accounts: unredeemed points are a liability to your customers. Agree with your accountant how to record them and how they affect taxes. To check whether the rate fits your margin, run the numbers through unit economics.

Cashback mechanics: 6 rules

1. The rate

A small rate is often enough. For customers, the feeling of "I have points on my balance" matters more than the number itself. Set the rate from your margin and don't start too high: cutting it later upsets people, while raising it is good news.

2. Which purchases earn points

Don't award points on discounted items or on the part of a receipt paid with points; otherwise you give double benefit on one item. Some categories (cigarettes, very low-margin goods) may earn no points at all — spell this out in the rules.

3. Redemption cap

Let points cover only part of a receipt, for example up to 20–30% (illustrative). That way every redemption still brings in cash.

4. Expiry

Points need an expiry date, or the liability piles up for years. Match it to purchase frequency: a few months for a coffee shop, longer for a clothing store. Send a reminder before points expire: "You have 15,000 points, valid until 31 December". That message often outperforms any promotional broadcast.

5. Tiers

Spend more, earn a higher rate — three tiers, for instance. Tiers give customers a goal, but the rules must fit in one sentence. If staff can't explain the tiers, customers won't understand them either.

6. Welcome bonus

A one-off sign-up bonus brings people back for a second purchase. Make it redeemable on the second purchase, not the first — the goal is the return visit.

How to set cashback up technically

You don't need a plastic card. The phone number is the ID. Options:

  • a loyalty module in your till software — many POS systems have one, so check yours first;
  • a Telegram bot — balance, history, reminders. Customers can't forget the card because it's on their phone;
  • a CRM — for service businesses: who came when and how many points they have.

A phone number is personal data: consent and storage rules are in our article on customer databases and personal data. Be clear when you announce the cashback terms too: rate, expiry, limits — the wording in ads and at the till should match.

How to tell customers and remind them

Even the best programme fails if nobody knows about it. Three touchpoints:

  • At the till. Staff say one sentence to every customer: "Shall I add your number? You'll get 5% back from this purchase." Put it in the script and check it daily.
  • On the receipt. On the receipt or in the bot: "You earned 8,000 points today, balance 23,000."
  • In messages. Expiring points, birthdays, new arrivals. But don't send ads every day or people will block the bot. How much is too much is covered in our Telegram bot broadcast rules.

Abuse: what can happen at the till

Cashback behaves like money, so it can be stolen like money. Common cases:

  • staff enter their own number on receipts of customers who didn't give one;
  • one customer claims the welcome bonus again and again with different numbers;
  • points aren't reversed when goods are returned.

Protection is simple: points are credited only after the customer confirms via the bot or SMS, returns cancel points automatically, and once a month you review the numbers with the largest accruals.

How to measure results

The point of cashback is repeat purchases. So the key question is: do members come back more often than everyone else?

MetricWhat it shows
Share of receipts in the programmeWhether staff are offering it
Redemption rateWhether customers remember the programme
Member return frequencyWhether the programme does its job
Average receipt: members vs othersWhether points lead to extra spending
Programme cost as % of revenueWhether the economics hold
Unredeemed points balanceHow much liability has built up

One caveat: members are more loyal customers to begin with, and may have come often before the programme. So compare the same customers before and after launch, not just members against everyone else.

Common mistakes

  • Rate too high. A few months in, the programme loses money and has to be shut down.
  • Points on discounted goods. Double benefit.
  • Points that never expire. The liability grows for years.
  • Complicated rules. "Tuesdays only, from 300,000 som, new collection only" — nobody joins.
  • Staff don't offer it. The programme exists, but few receipts go through it.
  • No reminders. Points quietly expire and customers forget the programme exists.

Questions and answers

What cashback rate is right?

There is no universal number. Start from your margin: the cost of cashback (at cost of goods, allowing for the redemption rate) should be a small part of the margin per receipt. It is safer to start low and adjust after three months based on results.

My bank already offers cashback. Do I need my own?

Bank or payment-app cashback ties the customer to the card, not to your shop. Your own cashback brings customers back to you specifically and gives you the customer base. Joining bank promotions is a separate decision: they often bring new customers but don't bring them back.

Can I combine cashback with a discount?

Yes, but not on the same receipt. The usual rule: discounted items earn no points. "Double points" during a promotion cost less than a discount and still bring people back.

How do I close the programme or change the terms?

Announce it in advance and give customers time to use their points. Wiping points without warning destroys trust and invites negative reviews.

Conclusion

Cashback works where purchases repeat and there is enough margin. Use it instead of a discount, set the rate from your margin, don't award points on discounted goods, add a redemption cap and an expiry date, tell customers at the till, on the receipt and in reminders, and measure how often members return.
