Premium segment marketing: how to sell an expensive product

Business owners selling a premium product often market it like a mass-market one: promotions, "30% off", "the lowest price" — and then wonder why leads come in but leave saying "too expensive". The problem is that premium buyers look for something else. They don't assess the price — they assess what they're paying for. Here's how premium marketing differs from mass-market marketing and how to sell an expensive product.

What a premium buyer is buying

In the mass market, people buy a product: a sofa, a course, a service. In the premium segment, they buy product + feeling + assurance:

  • Quality and detail — materials, finish, durability.
  • Peace of mind — confidence that everything will be done right, without hassle.
  • Service — a personal approach, speed, attention.
  • Status and taste — what their choice says about them.

That's why "cheap" doesn't work in premium marketing — it actually raises suspicion: "why so cheap? what's the catch?"

1. Show value, not price

Without context, a premium price always looks high. So show the value before the price:

  • What it includes: "This sofa's price includes a beech frame, a 15-year warranty, free delivery and assembly" (illustrative example).
  • Cost over time: "Lasts 10 years — that's ... soum a year."
  • Compare with the alternative: with a more expensive solution, not a cheaper one.

Whether to show the price in ads is a separate question — see should you show the price in your ads.

2. Details are the language of premium

In premium creatives, close-ups work better than wide shots: stitching, fabric texture, metal fittings, packaging. Detail shows quality without words.

The creative style is different too:

  • little text, lots of space;
  • calm music or natural sound — more in sound in video ads;
  • unhurried editing;
  • natural light, clean backgrounds.

A cheap-looking creative makes even a premium product look cheap.

3. Service is part of the product

For premium customers, service is inseparable from the product. If they submit a lead for a 100-million-soum kitchen and hear back three hours later, they'll go elsewhere. The premium service standard:

  • reply to leads as fast as possible, with a dedicated manager;
  • a personal conversation, not a template;
  • an invitation to a meeting, the showroom or a home visit;
  • contact after the purchase.

On response speed — lead response time.

4. Discounts kill premium

For a premium brand, constant discounts are the most dangerous tool. Every discount tells customers "the real price is lower". Next time they'll wait for one.

Instead of discounts:

  • added value: free installation, an extended warranty, a gift accessory;
  • limited collections: "only 10 pieces";
  • private offers: personal offers for loyal customers.

More in the discount trap.

5. Trust: selected clients and process

In the premium segment, "over 1,000 customers" carries less weight. What works better:

  • selected projects: a detailed story for each;
  • process: how it's made and by whom;
  • expertise: the owner or lead specialist on camera — more in founder on camera;
  • customer testimonials: named, with results — customer testimonial video.

6. Ad audiences: breadth and quality

The biggest mistake when advertising a premium product is narrowing the audience too much by hunting for "high income" interests. Such interests are often unreliable and shrink the audience needlessly.

A better approach:

  • let the creative filter the audience — premium style and price range;
  • lookalike audiences based on existing customers — lookalike audiences;
  • judge leads by sales, not by cost per lead.

It's normal for premium leads to cost more than mass-market ones. What matters is cost per sale.

Common mistakes

  • Mass-market creative: bright colours, a big "-30%".
  • Constant promotions.
  • Answering leads with a standard script.
  • Quoting the price with no context.
  • Judging ads by cost per lead alone.

Questions and answers

Can premium products be sold on Instagram?

Yes, but Instagram is usually the place of first contact. The purchase typically happens after a personal consultation, a showroom visit or a meeting. So the ad's goal is to get people to that meeting.

Are premium leads more expensive?

Usually, yes: the audience is smaller and decisions slower. But with a large ticket size, cost per sale can still be profitable.

Should I never discount?

Very rarely, and with a reason: a collection change, one event a year. Constant discounts destroy a premium position.

Can a small business be premium?

Yes. Premium is about attention to quality, service and detail, not company size. A small workshop can be premium too.

Conclusion

Premium marketing means value instead of price, detail instead of promotions, personal service instead of scripts. Creative, sales and service must be at the same level: one cheap-looking element spoils the whole impression.
