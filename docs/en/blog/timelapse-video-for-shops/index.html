A timelapse video shows several hours of work in 15–20 seconds. It suits a shop particularly well: the window gets dressed, new stock goes on the shelves, an order is put together — all of that happens anyway, you only need to put the phone in the right place. This article compares timelapse, hyperlapse and before–after, shows how to work out the speed and gives you three ready scripts.

What a timelapse video is and why a shop needs one

In a timelapse the camera captures frames rarely and plays them back at normal speed. A long process gets compressed: an empty window fills up in front of you, a shelf "stocks itself".

For a small business the format has three advantages:

  • No separate shoot day. The work is being done anyway — you just record it.
  • No talking. For owners who dislike speaking to camera, this is the easiest video there is.
  • The effort becomes visible. A customer sees the finished window but has no idea it took three hours. A timelapse shows it.

There is one limit: a timelapse does not sell on its own. It attracts attention and shows the shop is alive. To sell, you need a concrete shot at the end — the finished result, the product, a price or the address.

Timelapse, hyperlapse and before–after: which one when

These three formats are often mixed up. The difference is simple: does the camera move, and is the process itself shown.

FormatCameraWhat you seeGood forDifficulty
TimelapseStays stillThe whole process, sped upWindow dressing, stocking shelves, assembly, cookingEasy: a tripod and patience
HyperlapseMoves slowlyA "fly-through" of a spaceWalking into the shop, a showroom tour, from the street to the windowMedium: steady hands needed
Before–afterTwo shots from one spotOnly the start and the endThe process is long, dull or cannot be shownEasiest
Sped-up regular videoEitherThe process; you choose the speed in editingShort jobs (up to 20–30 minutes) where you need an exact speedEasy, but eats storage

Phones name the mode differently. On the iPhone camera it is called Time-lapse. On Samsung phones the mode is called Hyperlapse, although it shoots an ordinary timelapse too: according to Samsung's help page, the mode creates a time-lapse video, adjusts the frame rate automatically to what you are recording, and is available only on the rear camera and only on some devices (Samsung Support). Whether you can pick the speed by hand depends on the model — check on your own phone.

How to choose? A short rule:

  • the process looks good and fits in one fixed frame → timelapse;
  • you want to show the space, not the process → hyperlapse;
  • the process is messy, there are many strangers in frame, or the job runs over two days → before–after.

How to shoot a timelapse on a phone: setup and speed maths

Do the maths first

For Reels, 10–20 seconds of finished timelapse is enough. From that, a simple division gives the speed-up you need: length of the job ÷ length of the finished video.

How long the job takesFinished videoSpeed-up
10 minutes (packing an order)20 seconds30×
30 minutes (stocking a shelf)20 seconds90×
2 hours (dressing the window)20 seconds360×
6 hours (rearranging the shop)20 seconds1,080×

This is plain arithmetic, not a phone setting. In automatic mode many phones choose the speed themselves, and the clip may come out longer or shorter than you expected. So the day before an important shoot, run a 10-minute test and see how many seconds you get. If needed, you can speed it up further in editing.

7 things to sort out before you press record

  1. A tripod or a solid support. If the phone shifts by a millimetre, the timelapse shows it as a jump. No tripod — use a shelf, a box, tape.
  2. Vertical frame, 9:16. For Reels and Stories, stand the phone upright.
  3. Frame wider than you think. People move around, a stepladder appears — leave room at the edges.
  4. Power. On a long shoot, plug the phone in or use a power bank.
  5. Storage. Check free space first, especially if you plan to shoot regular video and speed it up later.
  6. Calls and notifications. Turn on Do Not Disturb or airplane mode — an incoming call can stop the recording.
  7. Light. Start in daylight and finish in the evening, and the frame slowly darkens. Either use that on purpose (the window lights come on — a great ending) or fit the job into consistent light.

The basics of light and framing are covered in shooting Reels on a phone.

3 ready scripts for a shop

Script 1. Dressing the window — timelapse

The most seasonal option: in late November many shops open their New Year window.

TimeShotOn-screen text
0–2 sThe finished, lit window (regular video)"This window took 3 hours"
2–14 sTimelapse: from empty glass to the finished display—
14–18 sThe central item up close, price cardProduct name and price
18–20 sWide shot from the streetAddress or "Open daily 10:00–21:00"

The trick is that the video starts with the result. If the first frame is empty glass, people scroll past. Show the outcome first, then how it was made. Ideas for the first seconds are in 10 Reels hooks.

Script 2. New stock arrived — before–after plus a short timelapse

Works for clothing, shoes, tableware and building-supply shops.

  • Shot 1 (2 s): a pile of closed boxes. Text: "The new collection is here".
  • Shot 2 (6–8 s): timelapse — boxes open, the shelf fills up.
  • Shot 3 (2 s): the finished shelf from the same spot — the "after" shot.
  • Shot 4 (4–5 s): the 3 most interesting products up close, each with a price.
  • Shot 5 (2 s): "In store from today" and the address.

The "before" and "after" shots must be taken from exactly the same spot. Leave the tripod where it is or mark its place on the floor with tape.

Script 3. Putting an order together — a hands timelapse

Works for florists, gift shops, bakeries, furniture shops and online stores. The camera looks at the table from above or from the side; only hands and product are in frame.

  • 0–2 s: the finished bouquet or gift set in a customer's hands (no face visible).
  • 2–12 s: timelapse — from an empty table to the finished set.
  • 12–16 s: the finished product turning slowly (normal speed).
  • 16–20 s: text: price range and how to order.

The advantage of this script is that there are no faces in frame, so the consent question gets much simpler. In a furniture shop the same template fits assembling a sofa or a wardrobe.

Editing: turning a timelapse into a Reel

A raw timelapse is not a Reel yet. Four things are added to it.

A beginning and an end. As in the scripts above: result → process → product → call to action. Let the timelapse itself take a little over half of the video.

Text. For people watching without sound, 2–3 short captions are enough. Keep them away from the edges: for Reels ads Meta recommends leaving 14% at the top, 35% at the bottom and 6% on each side free (Meta Ads Guide). Follow the same rule in organic Reels and your caption will not end up under the buttons.

Music. A timelapse has no original sound, so music is almost mandatory. Which music a business account and an ad can use is explained in music for video ads.

Even out the pace. If there was a long break in the process (everyone went to lunch), cut that part out. Which app makes this easiest — see the video editing apps overview.

Who is in the frame: people, neighbours, prices

A timelapse camera runs for hours, and things you did not plan end up in the shot.

  • Staff. They should know in advance that they will be in the video and agree to it. Using a person's image in advertising without their consent is prohibited in Uzbekistan — the procedure is in photo and video consent for advertising.
  • Customers and passers-by. The easiest way is to shoot when the shop is closed or before it opens. If that is not possible, set the camera at an angle where faces are not visible.
  • Neighbouring shops. Other people's signs and brands should not sit in the centre of the frame.
  • Old prices and promotions. Price cards caught in the shot must still be correct on the day the video goes out.
  • Till, documents, screens. A computer screen, a customer list, the safe — keep them out of frame.

6 common mistakes

  • The phone moved. Someone bumped the tripod and the whole recording is ruined. Mark the spot and warn the team.
  • The video starts with the process. Empty glass stops nobody. Result first.
  • Too long. Few people watch a 40-second timelapse to the end. 10–15 seconds of process is enough.
  • The battery or storage ran out. The recording stopped halfway and cannot be reshot.
  • No ending. The process ends — and so does the video. No product, price or address shown.
  • Shooting without a test. Recording a once-a-year job (the New Year window) without a trial run is a gamble.

Questions and answers

Do I need a special app for a timelapse?

In most cases no: the phone's own camera has this mode. A dedicated app helps when you need to set an exact speed or manage a recording that runs for several hours. For short jobs you can shoot regular video and speed it up in editing.

Can a timelapse be used in ads?

Yes, but not as the whole ad. The timelapse is the attention-grabbing part; the product and the offer should follow. Before launching, check the music rights and the consent of anyone in frame.

What if the job takes two days?

There are two ways. The first is to put the phone in exactly the same spot each day and join the two recordings in editing; mark the tripod position for that. The second is to drop the timelapse and do a before–after: two shots from one spot.

How much content comes out of one timelapse?

At least three pieces: the full Reel, a short 5–7 second clip for Stories and a before–after photo pair (stills from the video). On top of that, a photo of the finished result is useful for your map listing.

Conclusion

A timelapse video on a phone is the cheapest "process" content a shop can make: the work is done anyway, you put the phone on a tripod and work out the speed in advance. If the process looks good — timelapse; if you need to show the space — hyperlapse; if the job is long or messy — before–after. Whichever you choose, start the video with the result and end with the product, the price or the address.
