The discount trap: how to learn to sell without promotions

"Sales are down — let's run a promotion." The reflex is so natural that for many businesses discounts become the main marketing tool. The first promotion works, the second less so, and after the third, customers stop buying at full price. That's the discount trap: the business gets hooked on promotions, profit shrinks and the brand becomes "cheap". Here's how businesses fall into it, what a discount really costs and how to get out.

How the trap works

  1. Sales drop — the business announces a discount.
  2. Sales rise — conclusion: "discounts work".
  3. The promotion ends — sales fall below where they were: customers simply bought earlier.
  4. Another discount is needed.
  5. Customers learn: "if I wait, there'll be a discount".

In the end, the business has effectively cut its price without admitting it: the "real" price is now the sale price.

What a discount really costs

Many owners count a discount against revenue, but it comes out of profit. Illustrative example:

No discount20% discount
Price1,000,000 soum800,000 soum
Cost of goods and expenses700,000 soum700,000 soum
Profit per sale300,000 soum100,000 soum
Sales needed for the same profit1030

A 20% discount cut profit by two-thirds. To earn the same, sales must triple. Most promotions come nowhere near that — so they lose money; nobody just does the maths.

More on calculating profit — unit economics.

When a discount is right

Discounts aren't a bad tool — they're a misused one. They make sense:

  • for clearing stock: the season is over, the collection changes;
  • for winning new customers when repeat purchases are high — the loss on the first purchase is recovered later. More in repeat sales and LTV;
  • for filling quiet time: off-peak hours in a salon, restaurant or clinic;
  • for a clear, rare event: once or twice a year, one customers look forward to.

The problem starts when discounts become a permanent tool.

The "old price" and the law

Some businesses show discounts with a struck-through price: 1,500,000 crossed out next to 990,000. If the old price was never real, that's misleading advertising. The Law "On Advertising" prohibits unfair (misleading) advertising (Article 5, lex.uz) and requires the terms of prize promotions to be announced in advance (Article 21). Getting out of discounts: 4 stages

If customers are already used to discounts, stopping abruptly can crash sales. Exit in stages:

  1. Reduce discount frequency. Quarterly, not monthly.
  2. Replace discounts with added value. Instead of "20% off", offer "free delivery and installation". Similar benefit for the customer, cheaper for you.
  3. Explain the value. Show in your content what the price consists of, plus quality and service. Working with objections — content from customer objections.
  4. Reward loyal customers separately. Not a discount for everyone — a private offer for the loyal ones.

The process takes a month or two. Sales may dip a little in the first month, but profit and brand recover.

Sell with a reason, not a price

Dependence on discounts usually reveals one thing: customers don't know why they should buy from you specifically. So the real work is strengthening the offer:

  • is your unique selling proposition clear — USP;
  • do customers see the value;
  • does your service differ from competitors'.

Common mistakes

  • Counting a discount against revenue instead of profit.
  • A promotion every month.
  • A fake "old price".
  • Not evaluating a promotion together with the month after it.
  • Mass-market promotions on a premium product — more in premium segment marketing.

Questions and answers

What if competitors run promotions all the time?

If you compete with them on price, you both lose profit. Compete on other arguments: service, guarantees, speed, quality.

Are holiday promotions harmful too?

Not if they happen a few times a year and are planned in advance. The problem is constant, uncalculated promotions. Seasons — marketing calendar 2027.

How do I choose added value instead of a discount?

Find something valuable to the customer and cheap for you: delivery, installation, a consultation, an accessory.

How do I know a promotion paid off?

Compare profit for the promotion period plus the following month with the previous period. Don't look only at sales during promotion week.

Conclusion

A discount is a strong medicine that turns into poison when taken constantly. It eats profit, trains customers to wait and cheapens the brand. Use discounts rarely and with a calculation behind them; use added value, tiers and guarantees instead.
