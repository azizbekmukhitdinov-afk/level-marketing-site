What an advertising report should include

Many business owners get a month-end report full of pretty charts that still doesn't tell them whether the ads are profitable. A good report is a decision-making tool. Here are the numbers and sections every advertising report must include.

Report structure

  1. Period and goal — which month, and what the plan was.
  2. Key numbers — the table below.
  3. Breakdown by campaign and creative — what worked and what didn't.
  4. What was done — tests, changes, new creatives.
  5. Conclusions — what the numbers mean.
  6. Next month's plan — specific actions and expected results.

Key numbers

MetricWhat it shows
SpendHow much went on ads
Reach and impressionsHow many people saw the ads
Clicks and CTRHow interesting the creative is
Number of leads and CPLThe cost of one lead
Qualified leadsHow many are real prospects
Sales and revenueData from the CRM
Cost per saleAds + fees ÷ sales
ROMIWhether marketing pays off

For the last three rows, the client and agency have to share data: only the sales team knows which leads became sales. For calculating ROMI, see our ROMI article; for CPL, our CPL guide.

Red flags

  • The report shows only reach, likes and followers.
  • Spend isn't broken down by campaign.
  • Numbers aren't compared with last month.
  • No conclusions and no next steps.
  • The report is always late.

How the report is delivered

Sending numbers isn't enough — they need explaining. At Level Marketing, every month on the project date our specialist visits the client's office, walks through reach, leads and cost per lead, and discusses next month's plan.

A one-page report example (illustrative numbers)

  • Period: October. Plan: 300 leads a month.
  • Spend: $800.
  • Leads: 320, CPL $2.50 (September: $3.10).
  • Qualified leads: 190 — 59%.
  • Sales: 38, cost per sale from ads about $21.
  • What worked: customer testimonial video — CPL $1.90.
  • What didn't: carousel — CPL $4.20, paused.
  • November plan: three new testimonial-style videos, most of the budget on the best audience, lead response time cut to 30 minutes.

A report like this takes two minutes to read, and the decision is obvious.

How to read a report: 5 questions

  1. How did CPL change versus last month, and why?
  2. What share of leads is qualified — has it been checked with the sales team?
  3. Which creative and audience delivered the cheapest sale?
  4. Has the budget been moved to the campaigns that work?
  5. Does next month's plan include a specific numeric goal?

If the report doesn't answer these questions, ask your specialist for more detail.

Agree on the report up front

Before work starts, agree on a report template: which metrics, which period and on what day it's delivered. On your side, share the number of sales from your CRM every week — otherwise the ads get judged on leads alone and the conclusions will be wrong. For choosing a CRM, see What a CRM is and which one suits a small business.

Questions and answers

How often should you get a report?

The main report — once a month. With a large budget or after launching a new campaign, a short weekly snapshot also helps: spend, leads and CPL.

If CPL goes up, should you stop the ads right away?

Don't rush. Find the cause first: has the creative worn out, has the audience narrowed, has the season changed? Often a new creative or a broader audience is enough.

What if the report has no sales data?

Then there's no data flow between ads and sales. Start tagging leads in your CRM and sending the weekly sales count to your specialist — only then can you judge ad performance properly.

Conclusion

A good report answers three questions: how much was spent, how many sales came in and what changes next month. If yours doesn't, ask for it. This matters when choosing a specialist too — see how to choose a paid ads specialist.
