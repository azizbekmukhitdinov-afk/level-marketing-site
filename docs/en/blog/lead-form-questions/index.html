Lead form questions: using the form to filter out junk leads

Every business using instant forms eventually complains: "We get plenty of leads, but half don't answer the phone and the rest don't remember why they signed up." Often the problem isn't the ad but the form itself: it's so easy to fill in that people tap without thinking. Lead form questions are the cheapest way to filter out junk leads. Here's how to build the form.

A form is a contract: the easier it is, the lower the quality

Meta pre-fills the form: name and phone number come from the profile. Two taps and the lead is done. That boosts lead volume, but also the number of "I tapped by accident" leads.

Every extra question is a small barrier. More barriers mean fewer leads, but the people who submit are more serious. The goal is to place the barriers well: they shouldn't stop an interested person, but should stop a random one.

First decide whether a form or messages suit you better: lead form or messages.

Higher intent: a form with a review screen

When creating a form in Meta there are two main types: More volume and Higher intent. With Higher intent, people see a screen to review their details before submitting (Meta Business Help). Meta's developer documentation also describes this setting as a way to get higher-quality leads (Meta for Developers).

In practice, the review screen is one extra step that cuts accidental taps. If most of your leads are "empty", the first thing to do is switch on this type and compare for 1–2 weeks.

Which questions improve quality

A good question does two things: it stops a random person and gives the salesperson something to open the conversation with.

Question typeExample (illustrative)What it gives you
Need"Which room do you need furniture for?"The salesperson opens the call precisely
Timing"When are you planning to buy: this month / in 1–3 months / not sure yet"Separates hot leads from cold ones
Budget"Approximate budget: up to ... / ... to ... / over ..."Shows in advance who the price won't suit
Location"Which city or district do you live in?"Filters out areas you can't serve
Contact method"How should we reach you: call / Telegram"Reduces the "doesn't pick up" problem

Multiple-choice questions beat short-answer ones: they're easy to fill in on a phone and the answers land neatly in the CRM.

How many questions is optimal

There's no universal number, but here's a practical rule:

  • Cheap, quick purchase (flowers, a café table, delivery): name + phone + 1 question.
  • Mid-range ticket (courses, a clinic, a salon): name + phone + 2–3 questions.
  • Expensive purchase (real estate, custom furniture, cars): name + phone + 3–4 questions + Higher intent.

Expect cost per lead to rise as you add questions. That's fine — what matters is whether cost per qualified lead and cost per sale go down. How to measure quality — the cheap leads trap.

The rest of the form

Intro screen

State clearly at the start what's on offer: "Free measurement and a 3D design — a manager will call within 30 minutes" (illustrative example). If people know what happens next, they pick up.

Thank-you screen

This screen is often left empty. Use it: "Our manager Aziza will call you within 30 minutes from +998 ... Don't want to wait? Message us on Telegram." When people know which number will call, they answer even an unknown number.

Privacy policy

Meta requires a privacy policy link on the form. Rules for handling data — customer database and personal data.

After the form: do the answers reach the salesperson?

The most common loss: good questions, but the answers never reach the salesperson. The form is connected to the CRM, but only name and phone come through. The salesperson calls and asks again what the person already answered.

Check:

  • whether each answer shows up as a separate field on the CRM card;
  • whether salespeople read those answers before calling;
  • whether "timing: this month" leads get handled first.

If the numbers in the ad account and the CRM don't match — ad leads vs CRM mismatch.

Common mistakes

  • Asking everything: 8 questions, and nobody finishes the form.
  • Too many open questions: "Tell us about yourself" — no one types that on a phone.
  • A budget question where only one option looks "right" — people pick the "right" answer, not the honest one. Keep the options neutral.
  • Changing the questions without comparing results against the previous form.
  • No next step on the thank-you screen.

Questions and answers

Will adding questions reduce leads?

Yes, usually, and cost per lead rises. But if the share of leads that become sales grows, the overall result improves.

Is Higher intent always better?

No. For cheap, quick purchases it can be an unnecessary barrier. Switch it on when lead quality is the problem and compare results.

Isn't it rude to ask about budget?

Not if the options are respectful. It saves the customer time too: the salesperson can offer a suitable option right away.

How often should the form change?

Wait at least 1–2 weeks after each change. Don't change the form and the creative at the same time, or you won't know what made the difference.

Conclusion

The lead form is the door between advertising and sales. Built with the right questions, it sends your team's time to real customers instead of empty conversations. Two or three questions on need, timing and location, Higher intent where needed, and answers that actually reach the CRM — that's enough.
