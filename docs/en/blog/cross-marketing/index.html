Cross-marketing: joint promotions with non-competing brands

Cross-marketing is a joint promotion between two businesses that don't compete but serve a similar audience. A coffee shop reaches a bookstore's customers, a gym reaches the clients of a healthy-meal delivery service, and both win new customers with almost no ad budget. This guide covers how to choose a partner, what to agree on and how to measure the result.

What cross-marketing is — and what it isn't

In cross-marketing, two sides swap resources: access to their customer base, space by the till, packaging, an Instagram page, a venue for an event. Usually no money changes hands — each side contributes its share.

Don't confuse it with other kinds of partnership:

FormatWhat happensWho pays whom
Cross-marketingTwo brands run a joint promotion and share audiencesUsually nobody; each covers its own costs
Instagram partnership postA creator's or brand's content is promoted from another pageThe advertiser pays the creator and Meta
Affiliate programmeA partner brings customers and earns a share of salesYou pay the partner, for results
Co-brandingTwo brands release a joint productBy agreement

Collab posts and running creator content as ads are covered in our article on Instagram partnership ads. The pay-for-results model is in our article on affiliate programmes. Cross-marketing is simpler than both: two business owners shake hands and reach out to each other's customers.

When cross-marketing makes sense

Cross-marketing won't solve everything, but it works well in three situations:

  • local businesses — a shop, cafe or salon in the same district or the same mall. The customers walk the same streets;
  • a new opening — you have no base yet, but your partner has a loyal one;
  • seasons — New Year, 8 March, back to school: people buy several things at once.

The main advantage is trust. People believe a recommendation from a coffee shop they know more than an ad from a stranger. But reach is limited to the size of your partner's base, so cross-marketing complements advertising rather than replacing it.

Choosing a partner: 5 criteria

A good partner is not just "a friend who's in business". Answer five questions:

  1. Is the audience the same? Age, income, location, interests. A premium beauty salon and a budget clothing store don't match, even though both serve women.
  2. Is there any competition? Even partial. A coffee shop and a patisserie may steal each other's dessert sales.
  3. Is the brand level similar? Your customers will judge your partner's service through your name. If the partner lets them down, it hurts you too.
  4. Is there a purchase sequence? The best pairs are where one purchase ends and the next begins: wedding dress and photographer, renovation and furniture, gym and sports nutrition.
  5. What resources does the partner bring? Their Telegram channel, customer base, till space, content quality. "Keen to collaborate" is not enough.

6 cross-marketing formats

1. Voucher swap

The simplest format. The coffee shop gives each customer a small bookstore voucher; the bookstore hands out an invitation for coffee. Each voucher carries an expiry date and a promo code.

2. Joint bundle

Products from both brands in one set: flowers plus sweets, sportswear plus a first training session. The bundle is sold in both places and promoted on both pages.

3. Packaging and the till

The partner's flyer or card in your packaging or by your till. Cheap, but it only works with a concrete offer: "First workout free with this card", not a vague "discount at our partner".

4. Joint event

A workshop with a designer at a furniture store, an author meet-up at a bookstore with coffee from the partner. How to plan one is covered in our article on event marketing.

5. Shared content

A joint Reel, posts in each other's Telegram channels, a joint giveaway. If you run a giveaway, check the mechanics and the legal requirements first.

6. Co-branded product

The most complex format: a special coffee blend, a product carrying both brands. Get there after several successful partnerships, not on your first cross-promotion.

The agreement: a one-page template

Even a friendly deal should be on paper, or a month later you'll be arguing about who did more. Fit the following on one page:

  • the goal and dates of the promotion;
  • what each side provides: number of posts, flyers, gifts, discounts;
  • who pays for the discount or gift;
  • the offer wording for customers — identical on both sides;
  • tracking method: a separate promo code or QR code for each side;
  • when and how you'll share results;
  • how logos and brand materials may be used;
  • how either side can end the promotion early.

Don't hand each other your customer databases. Each side messages its own customers. Passing phone numbers to a third party without consent may break personal data rules — more in our article on customer databases and personal data.

Your first cross-promotion: a 4-week plan

Keep the first promotion small — the goal is to test the partner and the format.

  1. Week 1: choose. List 3–5 potential partners, score them against the five criteria and meet the two best fits.
  2. Week 2: agree. Pick one format and one offer, write the one-page agreement, prepare promo codes or QR codes.
  3. Week 3: prepare. Flyers, vouchers or posts. Give staff on both sides the offer in one sentence — exactly what to say to customers at the till.
  4. Week 4: launch and track. Start the promotion and share promo-code numbers with your partner weekly.

When it ends, hold a 30-minute debrief: what worked, what didn't, what to change next time. If the results are good, plan the next seasonal promotion with the same partner.

How to measure results

Measuring cross-marketing is easy if you plan for it. The main tool is a separate promo code or QR code for each partner. How to create and track codes is covered in our article on promo codes.

After each promotion, record three numbers:

MetricHow to count
Offers distributedNumber of flyers or vouchers, or post reach
Promo codes redeemedFrom the till or CRM
New customersCame with a code and weren't in your base before

Illustrative example. A coffee shop handed out 500 vouchers; 40 were redeemed, 30 of them by new customers. If the vouchers and flyers cost 1.5 million som, each new customer cost 50,000 som. Compare that with your customer acquisition cost from ads. The numbers only illustrate the method.

One more thing: how many of those new customers came back a month later. If someone came for a one-off gift and never became a regular, the promotion was just an expense.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing a partner out of friendship. If the audiences don't match, even the best agreement won't work.
  • A vague offer. "Our partner has a discount" — how much, where, until when?
  • One-sided effort. You posted three times, your partner once. Without a written agreement, that's hard to raise.
  • No measurement. A shared code or no code at all — you can't tell who brought whom.
  • A one-off promotion. Cross-marketing works better after a few repeats, once customers remember the partnership.
  • Swapping databases. Legal risk and lost customer trust.

Questions and answers

Where do I find a cross-marketing partner?

Look around: businesses on the same street or in the same mall. Ask your customers, "Where do you go before and after us?" The answers give you a ready-made list.

What if the partner's base is smaller than ours?

That's fine if they bring something else: a good location, strong content, a specific audience. Write down what each side contributes so nobody feels short-changed.

Does cross-marketing involve payment?

Usually not — each side covers its own costs. If one side contributes more and asks for money, it's closer to buying advertising or an affiliate deal. Put that in writing too.

How often should we run cross-promotions?

With one good partner, once a quarter or around seasonal peaks. Too many partners at once blur your brand and make results hard to measure.

Conclusion

Cross-marketing is a joint promotion with a business that shares your audience but doesn't compete with you. Pick a partner using the five criteria, settle on one concrete format, write a one-page agreement, give each side its own promo code and track whether new customers return.
