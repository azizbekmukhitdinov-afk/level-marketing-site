Which language should your ads use: Uzbek, Russian or both

The first question a Tashkent business owner faces when launching ads is the language. Uzbek, Russian or both? Most people answer by gut feeling: "my customers mostly speak Russian, I think." But ad language is not a matter of taste. It affects cost per lead, lead quality and even legal compliance. Here is what the law requires, how the two audiences really differ and how to test the language properly.

What the law says

Under Article 6 of the Law "On Advertising", advertising in Uzbekistan is distributed in the state language, and a translation may be repeated in other languages (lex.uz). The translation must not distort the main meaning. In outdoor advertising, the translation goes below the Uzbek text, takes no more than 40% of the area and uses smaller lettering.

The law has no separate rule for targeted social media ads, so the practical advice is simple: keep an Uzbek line in your Russian ads too — in the headline, the offer or the subtitles. It is safer legally and clear to a bilingual audience. Other requirements are covered in advertising law in Uzbekistan.

This is not legal advice. For outdoor ads and large campaigns, check with a lawyer.

Language is an audience segment, not a translation

The main mistake is translating one ad word for word. In practice, the people who respond to Uzbek ads and to Russian ads are often different segments: their age, city, buying habits and trust signals differ.

For example, for a furniture store (illustrative example):

TraitRespond to Uzbek adsRespond to Russian ads
GeographyTashkent + regions, many leads from regionsMostly Tashkent city
DecisionOften a family decision, several daysOften faster, one person
QuestionsInstalments, delivery, warrantyMaterial, size, design
Trust signalStore address, many customers, a familiar facePortfolio, reviews, exact specs

Every business will have its own table. But the conclusion is the same: Uzbek and Russian ads should be planned as separate creatives, separate offers and separate audiences.

How the platform sees language

Meta lets you restrict an audience by language, but that filter relies on the phone's interface language. Many people use their phones in Russian or English but watch content in Uzbek — and vice versa. So the language filter often narrows the audience for no reason.

What usually works better: keep the audience broad and let the creative select the language. The algorithm learns who watched the Uzbek video to the end and who submitted a lead from the Russian one. Your job is to give it a strong creative in each language.

Testing two languages: 4 steps

1. Same offer, two creatives

In the first test, keep the offer identical (say, "free measurement") and vary only the language and tone. Otherwise you won't know what worked: the language or the offer.

2. Separate ad sets

Give each language its own ad set, or at least its own ads. Split the budget evenly and wait at least 7–10 days. Compare not cost per lead but cost per sale or per qualified lead.

3. A language tag in the CRM

Each lead should show which language ad it came from, via a UTM tag or form name. More in UTM tags. A common pattern: leads in one language are cheaper, while leads in the other turn into sales more often.

4. The salesperson's language

Check which language your team replies in. If a customer who came from an Uzbek ad gets a Russian template reply, conversion drops. Simple rule: reply in the language the customer wrote in.

When each language tends to win

There is no formula, but practice gives some direction:

  • Expanding into the regions — Uzbek is almost always the main language. Regional strategy is covered in advertising in the regions.
  • B2B and technical products use many Russian terms. Still, Uzbek copy with a Russian term can be a good mix.
  • Mass-market products (food, clothing, household services) — Uzbek creatives usually get broader reach.
  • Premium segment — quality and detail matter more than language. The creative must be equally strong in both.

Adapt, don't translate

Translating Russian copy into Uzbek is not enough. The difference between living copy and a translation shows in the first line. For a good adaptation:

  • write a separate hook for each language — jokes and idioms don't carry over;
  • use everyday words in Uzbek copy, not bookish ones;
  • write in Latin script, but remember people also search in Cyrillic — see Latin and Cyrillic in Uzbek search;
  • pay attention to who speaks in the video: a native speaker sounds more convincing.

Common mistakes

  • Running ads in one language only and concluding that "the Uzbek audience doesn't work", when the creative was weak.
  • Mixing two languages equally in one ad: the copy gets longer and is fully clear to nobody.
  • Narrowing the audience with a language filter for no reason.
  • Not tagging leads by language — then you can't tell which language pays off.
  • Keeping the profile and site in one language only: the ad is in Uzbek, the profile is all Russian, and people get lost.

Questions and answers

Is a Russian-language ad against the law?

The law says advertising is distributed in the state language and may be repeated in translation in other languages. There is no separate procedure for social media ads, so the safest path is to keep Uzbek text in Russian ads as well.

How should the budget be split between languages?

Start evenly, compare by sales for 2–3 weeks, then give more to the language that performs better. Don't switch the second language off completely — its audience buys too.

Should the video be shot separately in each language?

Ideally yes, especially when a person talks to camera. For product videos, one video with two subtitle or voice-over versions also works.

Which language gives cheaper leads?

You can't tell in advance — it depends on the niche, creative and region. That's why language is tested, not guessed.

Conclusion

Ad language is a separate audience segment. The law makes the state language primary, while the market is bilingual. The best results come not from translating one ad but from a separate creative for each language and proper tracking.
