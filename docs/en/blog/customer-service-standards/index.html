Customer service standards: 10 rules and how to enforce them

Marketing brings customers to the door. After that, service decides everything: how the salesperson replied, how staff greeted them, how a problem was solved if one came up. One bad experience turns into a negative review and wipes out part of your ad budget. Here's how to write customer service standards and how to enforce them.

Why you need a standard

Without a standard, service quality depends on an employee's mood: great one day, poor the next. A standard is a set of written rules: what is done, when and how. It:

  • helps train new staff quickly;
  • makes quality measurable and controllable;
  • gives customers the same experience every time.

10 core rules

1. Response time

A specific time for each channel: for example, Telegram and DMs within 15 minutes during working hours, and phone calls answered within 3 rings (an illustrative example). More in lead response time.

2. Greeting and introduction

A name, the company and an offer to help. Not robotic, but with a consistent structure.

3. Use the customer's name

Ask for their name and use it — it makes the interaction feel personal.

4. Answer the question directly

To "How much is it?", give the price or a range first, then ask a clarifying question — replying to "how much?".

5. Keep promises

"I'll call tomorrow" means a call tomorrow. Every promise is logged as a task in the CRM.

6. Language and tone

Reply in whatever language the customer writes in. Polite but plain, without jargon.

7. Handling complaints

Don't argue. The sequence: listen → apologise → offer a solution → give a deadline → check the result.

8. Sign-off and next step

Every conversation ends with a concrete next step: "Your order will be delivered tomorrow at 14:00."

9. Follow-up after purchase

A few days later: "Is everything all right?" It catches problems early and is the right moment to ask for a review — collecting customer feedback.

10. Data security

Customer data lives only in the CRM, not on employees' personal phones, in line with the personal data law — customer database and personal data.

How to write the standard

  1. Map the customer journey from first message to post-purchase follow-up — customer journey map.
  2. Set a rule for each stage: what's done, how fast and in what words.
  3. Prepare templates: answers to frequent questions, a reply to complaints.
  4. Keep it short: 2–3 pages, so staff actually read and remember it.

Enforcement: keep the standard off the shelf

  • Review calls and chats: analyse a few random conversations every week.
  • Mystery shopping: a friend or hired person contacts you as a customer.
  • Metrics: response time, review ratings, repeat purchases, number of complaints.
  • Customer ratings: a short post-purchase survey — NPS or a 1–5 score.

Finding unanswered conversations — unanswered Instagram DM audit.

Training staff

  • New employees are trained on the standard in their first week.
  • Once a month, the team reviews good and bad conversations together.
  • Recognition and bonuses for great service.

Questions and answers

Does a small business need a standard?

Especially a small one: with 2–3 employees, one person's bad day ruins the whole experience. The standard can fit on one page.

Won't templates sound robotic?

A template is a structure, not a word-for-word script. Staff adapt it in their own words.

How should we reply to a negative review?

Openly, calmly and with a solution — online reputation and reviews.

Conclusion

Customer service is the continuation of marketing: ads bring customers in, service keeps them. A written standard, ready templates, regular checks and training tie quality to a system rather than to an employee's mood.
