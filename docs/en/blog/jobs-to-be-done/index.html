Jobs to be Done: what job customers hire your product for

Most business owners describe their customer by age, gender and income: "women aged 25 to 35, middle income". Yet two women of the same age buy the same product for completely different reasons. The Jobs to be Done (JTBD) approach asks a different question: what happened in this person's life, and what "job" did they "hire" your product to do? This article treats JTBD as a working tool rather than a theory: interview questions, a formula and how to use it in advertising.

JTBD in short: customers "hire" products

The JTBD idea was popularised by Harvard professor Clayton Christensen. His article with co-authors, "Know Your Customers' Jobs to Be Done", appeared in the September 2016 issue of Harvard Business Review, and the book "Competing Against Luck" came out the same year.

The core idea is simple: people do not buy products, they "hire" them to make progress in their lives. In the definition used by the Christensen Institute, a job is the progress a person is trying to make as they move towards a goal, and it is always tied to specific circumstances.

So the question is not "who is my customer?" but "in what situation, and trying to achieve what, did they come to me?". This also changes your list of competitors: your competitor is any solution that does the same job, even one from a completely different category.

The milkshake story

The best-known JTBD example is a milkshake at a fast-food chain. As Harvard Business School's Working Knowledge describes it, the company first asked customers how to improve the shake and changed the product based on their answers. Sales did not grow.

A researcher working with Christensen then tried a different route. He spent a whole day in a restaurant, noting who bought a shake, when, and in what situation. It turned out that a large share of shakes were sold early in the morning, to go. The buyers were people facing a long, boring drive. For them the shake did several jobs at once: make the commute less dull, keep one hand busy, avoid messing up work clothes and hold off hunger until lunch.

The key conclusion: the morning shake's competitor was not another café's shake but a bagel, a doughnut and other food eaten on the road. The answer was not "make it tastier" but do this specific job better.

We do not add numbers to this story. It illustrates one point: the answer lies not in what customers say they need, but in their situation.

The three layers of a job

The Christensen Institute stresses that every job involves three kinds of forces:

  • Functional: the practical result. "Deliver it fast", "make the tooth stop hurting".
  • Social: how I look to others. "Not embarrassed in front of guests", "respected by colleagues".
  • Emotional: how I feel. "Calm", "sure I made the right choice".

Most advertising talks only about the functional layer. Yet decisions are often made for social or emotional reasons, and the functional reason simply helps to justify them.

A practical rule: the persona decides who sees the ad, JTBD decides what it says.

Interviews: whom to ask and what to ask

JTBD insight comes from conversations, not questionnaires. The most useful people to talk to are recent buyers (for example, within the last one to three months): they still remember how they decided. Also talk separately to people who considered you but chose someone else.

Ask about an event, not "what do you like?". Questions for a 20 to 30 minute conversation:

  1. When did you first think you needed a solution like this? What happened that day?
  2. How did you handle this problem before?
  3. Why did the old way stop being good enough?
  4. Which options did you consider, including "doing nothing"?
  5. Who did you ask for advice, and what did they say?
  6. What worried you before buying?
  7. At what moment did you make the final decision?
  8. What changed in the first days after buying?
  9. If we did not exist, what would you have done?
  10. How would you explain this result to a friend in your own words?

Write down the customer's exact words: those phrases later become your ad copy. If you have many written reviews, they can be mined too: analysing customer reviews with AI.

The job statement: the job in one sentence

After the interviews, reduce each recurring situation to a single sentence:

"When [situation], I want to [progress], so that [expected outcome or feeling]."

BusinessJob statement (illustrative example)
Dental clinicWhen my wedding is two weeks away, I want to fix my teeth quickly, so that I can smile freely in photos
English schoolWhen I have a call with a foreign partner at work, I want to be able to speak, so that I feel confident in front of my manager
Furniture storeWhen we move into a new flat, I want to furnish the kitchen within a month, so that we can host relatives comfortably

A good job statement contains no product name. If the sentence says "our course", you have described the solution, not the job.

Using JTBD in advertising

JTBD pays off fastest in the ad message. Build a separate creative approach for each job you find:

  • The hook starts with the situation: "A call with a foreign partner tomorrow, and you will stay silent again?" For more on hooks see 10 Reels hooks.
  • The main message promises progress, not features.
  • The proof shows what matters to someone in that exact situation.
  • The CTA is a lead form or a link to a page.

It also sharpens your unique selling proposition: you are not "the best overall" but the best fit for a specific situation.

Worked example (illustrative example)

An English school talked to 12 recently enrolled students. Three recurring jobs came up: speaking for work, applying to study abroad (IELTS), and keeping a child usefully busy after school. Before this, the school ran one generic ad: "English courses, experienced teachers".

In the new plan, a monthly budget of 6 million soums was split across three creative groups:

The average cost per lead fell a little, but the main gain is knowing which job is cheaper to reach and which converts into sales better. The next step is to track lead-to-payment conversion per job in the CRM and reallocate budget accordingly. Each such change is best written down as a hypothesis: marketing experiments and ICE.

Common mistakes

  • A job that is too vague. "The customer wants quality service" is not a job; everyone wants that.
  • Only asking current customers. People who did not choose you show more clearly who your real competitor is.
  • Asking about the future. Asked "would you buy?", people politely say yes. Ask about a past event.
  • Concluding from one interview. Until a situation repeats across several conversations, do not treat it as a job.
  • Not testing the result. A JTBD insight stays a hypothesis until it is tested in ads, for example with A/B testing ad creatives.

Questions and answers

Is JTBD only for startups and tech?

No. It works in any business: a clinic, a training centre, furniture, a car service. Understanding in what situation and why a person came to you is useful everywhere, and in services the message is especially easy to change.

How many interviews do I need?

There is no fixed number. In practice you can stop when new conversations stop producing new situations. For a small business, 8 to 15 conversations are often enough to see the main jobs, but treat that as a guide, not a rule.

Can I run the interviews myself?

Yes, but drop the sales tone. You are not selling anything, only listening to a story. Ask permission to record and do not "correct" the customer's answers.

Does JTBD replace the buyer persona?

No. The persona is needed for targeting and choosing channels; JTBD is for the message and product decisions. Together they give you ads that reach the right person with the right words.

Conclusion

JTBD is a way to see customers through "in what situation and why" rather than "who". Talk to recent buyers, ask about the event, turn recurring situations into job statements and test a separate ad message for each job. Instead of a generic ad, you get a message in which customers recognise themselves.
