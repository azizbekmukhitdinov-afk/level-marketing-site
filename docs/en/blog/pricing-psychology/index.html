Same service, same price, yet in one salon customers pick the expensive package and in another the cheapest one. Often the difference is not the price itself but how it is shown: which line comes first on the price list, how many options there are, how the number is written. In this article we go through practical rules for price tags, menus and price lists, explain why each one works, and where the line is that you should not cross.

How a buyer reads a price

Most people do not know the "real value" of a product. So a buyer compares the price with whatever is next to it: another option, an old price, a competitor, the amount they expected. In a 1974 paper, psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman described how people make estimates by starting from an initial number (an anchor) and not adjusting far enough away from it (Science, 1974).

The practical takeaway is simple: a price tag, menu or price list is a context for comparison. If you do not provide the context, the buyer will find one, usually at a competitor. The general principles of how the brain makes decisions are in our article on neuromarketing; here we deal only with price.

1. Prices ending in 9

199,000 soums looks noticeably cheaper than 200,000, even though the difference is only 1,000 soums. Research by Manoj Thomas and Vicki Morwitz found that this "left-digit effect" works mainly when the leftmost digit changes (Journal of Consumer Research, 2005).

That gives a concrete rule:

  • 2,000,000 → 1,990,000: the first digit changes, so the effect is possible.
  • 1,950,000 → 1,990,000: the first digit stays the same, and you simply lose 40,000 soums.

When to use it: products people compare on price, promotions and entry prices. When to be careful: premium services, gifts, luxury goods. There a round price may look more considered. This is not a law of nature, so test it with your own customers.

2. The anchor: the first price seen

The first price a buyer sees becomes the yardstick for everything after it. In practice:

  • Start the price list with the most complete package. After "full care package: 650,000", the line "basic care: 280,000" looks reasonable.
  • Put one expensive item in each menu section. Even if it rarely sells, it anchors the other prices in that section.
  • Let the salesperson open with the top option. The customer will step down to a more affordable one and feel it was their own choice.

The anchor has to be honest: the expensive option should genuinely sell and be worth its price. A price invented purely "for show" is manipulation.

3. The decoy

In 1982, Joel Huber, John Payne and Christopher Puto showed that when a third option is added to two others, and it is worse than one of them in every respect, people start choosing that dominating option more often (Journal of Consumer Research, 1982). This is called the decoy or attraction effect.

Illustrative example, a coffee shop menu:

SizePriceWhat the customer sees
Small (250 ml)18,000Cheap, but not much
Medium (350 ml)26,000The decoy
Large (450 ml)28,000"Only 2,000 more for 100 ml extra"

The medium size is there to make the large one look like good value. Services work the same way: a "Standard" package and a slightly more expensive "Business" package that gives a lot more. One essential condition: every option is real and the customer can buy any of them.

4. Bundles

A bundle does two things: it makes individual prices harder to compare and it raises the average order. One line, "manicure + pedicure + gel coating: 290,000", requires less mental arithmetic than three separate lines.

Two rules:

  • Calculate the bundle's margin. A discount inside a bundle eats margin fast, see the table in margin vs markup.
  • Build a logical bundle. Things that solve one task sell together: phone + case + screen protector. Padding the bundle with slow-moving stock damages trust.

More ways to sell extra items are in upselling and cross-selling.

5. How the price is displayed

The same number is perceived differently depending on how it is written. Things worth testing:

  • Splitting into a smaller unit. "99,000 a month" or "3,300 soums a day" sounds lighter than 1,188,000 a year. The total should still be visible.
  • Instalments. A monthly payment makes a large price manageable, but ads must show the terms in full, see installment advertising rules.
  • Font and placement. The price should not be the largest element; show what the customer gets first, then the price.
  • One format. Do not mix "199k", "199.000" and "199,000 soums" on one price list.
  • Currency. Showing prices in a foreign currency in advertising is prohibited (Law "On Advertising", Article 16, lex.uz). Keep prices in soums.

Whether to show the price in ads at all is a separate question, see should you show the price in your ads.

After: three packages at the top of the price list, individual services below.

Premium450,000Haircut + treatment + manicure + gel coating

If out of 100 customers 30 choose Basic, 55 Standard and 15 Premium, the average receipt is: 0.30 × 150,000 + 0.55 × 250,000 + 0.15 × 450,000 = 250,000 soums. This is a hypothesis. Test it for 4 weeks, take the actual split from your CRM and check each package's margin. If customers mostly end up in Basic, the value of Standard has not been explained well enough. Ethical limits and the law

Pricing psychology is there to make a decision easier, not to deceive. Test where the line is with a simple question: would the customer be upset if they knew everything?

  • Fake old price. A "crossed-out" price that was never actually charged is misleading advertising. The law defines false advertising as advertising that misleads consumers through inaccuracy, exaggeration or concealment of information (Article 5, lex.uz). More in advertising law in Uzbekistan and the discount trap.
  • Hidden fees. A "service charge" or "packaging fee" that appears at checkout fools the customer once and becomes the reason they never come back.
  • Fake urgency. A countdown timer that restarts every day, or a permanent "only 2 left".
  • Fake anchor. A "premium" option nobody can actually buy.

How to test

  1. One change at a time. If you change the price, the order and the number of packages at once, you will not know what worked.
  2. At least 2 to 4 weeks. Weekdays and paydays affect the result.
  3. Three numbers: conversion (buyers out of those who asked), average order and the share of each option.
  4. Check the margin. If the average order went up but profit went down, that is not a win.

Common mistakes

  • Using a 9-ending price where the leftmost digit does not change.
  • 30 equal lines on a price list: the customer cannot choose and takes the cheapest.
  • Packages that differ only in price, with no logical difference in what is included.
  • Hiding the price and "calculating individually" for every customer.
  • Using one technique everywhere: the rules for a premium salon and a discount store are different.

Questions and answers

Will a price ending in 9 make my brand look cheap?

Sometimes it can, especially in the premium segment. So use it for promotions and entry prices, test round prices on your core premium services, and compare the results.

How many options should a package line-up have?

Many businesses start with three: enough to compare, not so many that choosing gets hard. But it is not a magic number: if your customers clearly split into two groups, two options are enough.

Does this work for a small business?

Yes, and it is even easier in a small business: you can rewrite a price list or menu in a day and see the result at the till within the first weeks.

Does pricing psychology replace discounts?

Partly. A well-presented price reduces the need for discounts because the customer understands the value. But if the product is not worth its price, no price tag will save it.

Conclusion

Pricing psychology is about making a price easier to understand without lowering it. Use the left-digit effect where it works, build honest anchors and logical packages, show prices in soums in one consistent format, and measure every change.
