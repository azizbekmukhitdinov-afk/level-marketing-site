What is growth hacking: cheap growth experiments for small firms

Growth hacking is often sold as "one clever trick that doubles your sales". In reality it isn't magic; it's a discipline: run small experiments quickly, measure the result and keep what works. This article explains how growth hacking is structured, how to prioritise ideas with ICE scoring and which 10 low-cost experiments a small business can start right away.

What growth hacking is, and what it isn't

The term was popularised by Sean Ellis in 2010. He worked on the early growth of startups such as Dropbox and later co-wrote the book "Hacking Growth" with Morgan Brown. Ellis himself stresses that the point isn't tricks but a system of experiments (interview in Lenny's Newsletter).

Put simply, growth hacking comes down to three rules:

  • find the narrowest point in your growth: ads, sales or repeat purchases;
  • run small experiments every week, not one big project a month;
  • trust only numbers: not "I think it got better" but "enquiries went from 20 to 31".

What isn't growth hacking? Spam messages, fake reviews, bought followers and other rule-breaking. They produce numbers for a short while, and then the account gets blocked or your reputation suffers.

First, find the bottleneck

Many owners think growth only means new customers. But the problem is often somewhere else: there are enquiries, but nobody answers them; customers buy once and never come back. Split your funnel into five stages and write down one number for each.

StageQuestionMetric
AcquisitionWhere do people find you?Number and cost of enquiries
ActivationDo enquiries reach a first purchase?Enquiry-to-sale conversion
RetentionDo they buy again?Share of repeat purchases
ReferralDo they tell friends?Share of "heard from a friend"
RevenueHow much profit does a customer leave?Average order, margin

The stage with the weakest result is your bottleneck. Aim every experiment there first. To work out how much you can pay for advertising and which number matters, read our article on unit economics.

Illustrative example: a beauty salon in Tashkent gets 200 enquiries a month, but only 40 people book. The bottleneck here isn't advertising; it's response speed and the booking process.

The experiment loop: 4 steps

Growth hacking runs on a weekly rhythm. One 30–40 minute meeting a week is enough.

  1. Collect ideas. Ideas from the team, salespeople and customers go into one list. No idea gets rejected at this stage; it just gets written down.
  2. Prioritise. Each idea is scored with ICE, and the top 1–3 are chosen.
  3. Test. The chosen idea is tested with a deadline and a metric set in advance.
  4. Review. The result is recorded: worked, didn't work or unclear. What worked becomes a standard process; what didn't is closed.

The key is to record every experiment. After three months that list becomes your business's most valuable knowledge base: what works and what doesn't.

ICE scoring: how to prioritise ideas

ICE is three questions, each scored from 1 to 10:

  • Impact: if it works, how much will the bottleneck number change?
  • Confidence: how sure are you that it will work? Do you have data, or just a hunch?
  • Ease: how much time and money does it take?

Teams either average the scores or multiply them. Both versions are in use; what matters is applying the same one every time.

IdeaImpactConfidenceEaseAverage
Rule: reply to every enquiry within 5 minutes8877.7
"Bring a friend" bonus7586.7
Build a new website6424.0
Quiz in a Telegram bot5465.0

The scores in the table are illustrative. Notice that the "biggest" idea, a new website, ends up at the bottom: expensive, slow and uncertain. Cheap, fast ideas get tested first.

10 low-cost experiments for small businesses

Each needs a week or two and a small budget. Pick the ones that match your bottleneck.

Acquisition

  1. Test the offer. Run one ad with two different offers, such as "free measuring visit" versus "free design draft", keeping everything else in the creative the same.
  2. Lead magnet. Offer a price list, a buying guide or a calculator in exchange for contact details. How to do it is covered in our article on lead magnets.
  3. Maps listing. Refresh your photos on Yandex and Google Maps, ask for reviews and track calls and route requests for a month.

Activation

  1. Response speed. For one week, reply to every enquiry within 5 minutes and compare enquiry-to-sale conversion with the previous week.
  2. Make the first step easier. Instead of "call to find out the price", show the price on the page or add a "message us on Telegram" button.

Retention

  1. Reminders. A set time after a service, send the customer a reminder: a haircut, an oil change, a cleaning. What this does for revenue is explained in our article on repeat sales and LTV.
  2. A card in the package. A promo code for the next purchase or a QR code to your Telegram bot.

Referral

  1. A two-sided bonus. A gift for both the person who refers and the new customer. Dropbox giving extra storage to both the inviter and the invitee became the textbook example of this. How to set up such a programme is in our article on affiliate programmes.
  2. Timing the review request. Ask for a review and a referral when the customer is happiest: the day they see the result.

Revenue

  1. A checkout offer. An add-on that fits the main purchase: cushions with a sofa, a case with a phone. Track average order value for a month.

The experiment card

Before each test, write down five lines. The card stops you from "interpreting" the result generously afterwards.

  • Hypothesis: "If we do ..., metric ... will change by ..., because ..."
  • Metric: one main number
  • Timeframe: start and end dates
  • Success criterion: what result counts as "it worked"
  • Owner: who runs it and records the result

A filled-in card looks like this (an illustrative example for a training centre):

  • Hypothesis: if we send a reminder on the morning of the trial lesson, more of the people who signed up will attend, because many simply forget.
  • Metric: share of sign-ups who attend the trial lesson.
  • Timeframe: two weeks.
  • Success criterion: if attendance rises noticeably compared with the previous two weeks, the reminder becomes a permanent rule.
  • Owner: the administrator, who logs the result in the table every Friday.

A test like this costs almost nothing, but it hits revenue directly: someone who skips the trial lesson won't buy the course either.

How to read the result

One variable at a time. If you change the offer, the image and the price all at once, you won't know what worked. How to do this properly with ad creatives is covered in our article on A/B testing.

Enough volume. You can't conclude anything from ten enquiries. If volume is small, run the test longer or only trust a very large difference.

Don't stop early. Results in the first two days are often random. Wait for the deadline you set in advance.

Account for seasonality. Comparing a December experiment with January isn't fair. Where possible, compare two groups over the same period.

Common traps

  • Focusing only on acquisition. If the bottleneck is in sales, new ads just burn more money.
  • Too many experiments at once. A small team can run 1–3 tests a week properly.
  • Vanity metrics. More likes and followers aren't sales. Tie every experiment to a metric close to revenue.
  • Breaking the law or platform rules. Unsolicited SMS, fake reviews and misleading promotions lead to fines and bans.
  • Not recording results. An experiment that wasn't written down is an experiment that never happened.

Questions and answers

Is growth hacking only for startups?

No. The principles suit any business: find the bottleneck, run a small test, measure. A shop, a clinic or a training centre can work in weekly experiments too. The only difference is scale.

Do I need a dedicated growth hacker?

Not to start. It's enough for the owner or marketer to run the weekly meeting while salespeople and the media buyer supply ideas and data. What matters is one owner and an experiment log.

How long does one experiment take?

Usually one to two weeks. For purchases with a long decision cycle, such as furniture, property or education, results come slowly, so extend the test or measure an intermediate metric such as enquiries or meetings.

What if an experiment fails?

Record it and close it. A failed experiment is still a result: it saves you from spending money in the wrong direction. Most experiments fail, and that's how the system is supposed to work.

Conclusion

Growth hacking isn't a trick; it's a system: find the bottleneck in your growth, prioritise ideas with ICE, run a small experiment every week and record the result. The best experiments for a small business are cheap and fast: response speed, offer tests, reminders, referral bonuses and checkout offers.
