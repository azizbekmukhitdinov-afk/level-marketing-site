Brand archetypes: the 12 types and how to choose yours

Plenty of business owners describe their brand as "high quality, reliable, modern". The trouble is that the shop next door says exactly the same thing. Brand archetypes help you break out of that: they give a brand a character, and that character sounds the same everywhere, from a post caption to the way a sales rep answers the phone. This article covers the 12 archetypes, a 5-step way to choose one and the most common mistakes.

What a brand archetype is and where the idea comes from

An archetype is a figure people recognise across generations: the hero, the sage, the caring mother, the rebel, the joker. The Swiss psychiatrist Carl Gustav Jung brought the idea into psychology. Margaret Mark and Carol Pearson adapted it for marketing in their 2001 book "The Hero and the Outlaw", where they set out 12 archetypes and described each one as a brand character.

Put simply, an archetype answers the question: if your brand were a person, what kind of person would it be? How does it talk, what annoys it, what does it promise customers and in what tone?

It also helps to know what an archetype will not do. It won't give you a product, a price or a place in the market — that is the job of brand positioning and your USP. The archetype decides the "voice" in which that positioning is delivered.

  • stability and control — Caregiver, Creator, Ruler;

The examples are illustrative: one niche can hold several archetypes. Two coffee shops sell the same coffee, yet one is an Everyman (cheap, quick, round the corner) and the other is a Lover (atmosphere, décor, a place to meet).

How to choose your archetype: 5 steps

1. Write down what the customer wants to feel

Start with the emotion, not the product. A mother bringing her child to a clinic wants safety and calm — that's the Caregiver. A young man signing up at a gym wants to beat his own limits — that's the Hero. Talk to three or four customers and note the words they use.

2. Look at the founder and the team

A brand character shouldn't be fake. If the owner is a serious person who talks in numbers, a Jester brand won't last: the first time things get tough, it will slip back to its real voice. Whoever appears in the videos and answers the phone has to "play" the archetype every day without effort.

3. Map your competitors

Open the Instagram profiles of 5–7 competitors in your city and label each with a likely archetype. You'll often see the same picture: everyone is a Sage, or everyone is a Ruler. The empty spot is your opportunity. If most training centres in Tashkent talk about "results and certificates", a centre with an Explorer tone will stand out.

4. Pick one main archetype and one supporting one

The main archetype covers 70–80% of your messages. The supporting one adds colour. Take a furniture workshop: main — Creator (handmade, built to your measurements), supporting — Caregiver (for your home, safe for children). Three or more archetypes isn't a character, it's chaos.

5. Test it

Write ad copy for one product in two tones and run both on a small budget. An archetype is a hypothesis; the numbers will confirm or reject it. Read the comments too — the words people use to describe you are the best test.

One offer, three voices

To see the difference in practice, take one message: "Teeth whitening, discount before New Year". Three clinics would say it three ways.

  • Sage: "Does whitening damage enamel? Not if the right method is chosen. At the consultation we'll check your teeth and explain which option suits you."
  • Caregiver: "Worried about pain? We explain every step in advance and never rush you. Start the holidays with a calm smile."
  • Magician: "One session, a new smile. You won't recognise yourself in the New Year photos."

All three are fine, but each attracts a different customer. That's why you choose the archetype once and keep it in every post and ad. How to turn tone into a one-page rule is covered in our brand tone of voice guide.

An archetype isn't your brand's logo. It's how your brand behaves.

6 common mistakes

  • Taking every archetype at once. "We're caring, bold and funny" ends up as no character at all.
  • Choosing by dream rather than product. When a budget shop tries to be a Ruler, customers spot the gap between price and promise straight away.
  • Leaving it in the logo. The designer draws a fitting logo, but posts and sales reps speak a different language.
  • Misreading the Outlaw. A bold tone doesn't mean rudeness or breaking advertising law. Putting down competitors and using offensive language is off limits.
  • Changing it every month. An archetype works over years. A new character every season looks like a new brand to the customer.
  • Copying a global brand blindly. Big brands' tone rests on huge budgets and long histories. Borrow the idea, adapt the execution.

Questions and answers

Does a small business need an archetype, or is this for big brands?

A small business needs it even more. A large brand can drown out an off-key tone with ad spend; a small business can't afford a wasted post or call. An archetype speeds up decisions: an SMM manager or salesperson can answer "would our brand say this?" on their own.

Can I find my archetype with an online quiz?

Quizzes are useful to start the thinking, but they're not the final answer. They measure how you feel, not how your customers feel. Compare the quiz result with customer conversations and your competitor map.

Are archetype and positioning the same thing?

No. Positioning is the place you hold in the customer's mind: who you serve and why you're better. The archetype is the character with which you hold that place. Positioning first, archetype second.

Can I change the archetype during a rebrand?

Yes, if the business itself has changed: a new audience, a new price segment, a new product. But change it in stages — tone first, visuals second — and explain to existing customers what has changed.

Conclusion

Brand archetypes are a simple way to give a brand character. Start with what the customer wants to feel, check that the character fits the founder and the team, map your competitors, choose one main and one supporting archetype and test it in ads. Then keep that character everywhere, from the logo to the phone greeting.
