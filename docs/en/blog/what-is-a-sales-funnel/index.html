What a sales funnel is, in plain words

When someone complains that "ads don't work", the cause is often not the ads but another stage on the way to a sale. A sales funnel is the customer's path from first contact with you to purchase. Split it into stages and measure them, and you can see exactly where money is leaking.

Funnel stages

StageWhat happensMetric
ReachPeople saw an ad or postImpressions, reach
InterestThey clicked or visited the profileCTR, visits
LeadThey messaged or filled in a formNumber of leads, CPL
Qualified leadGenuinely ready to buyShare of qualified leads
SaleThey paidConversion, average order
Repeat purchaseThey came backLTV, share of repeat purchases

An example (illustrative numbers)

A furniture store showed ads to 50,000 people in a month:

  • 1% clicked → 500 visits;
  • 10% of them messaged → 50 leads;
  • 20% of leads bought → 10 sales.

Now improving any stage changes the result: if lead-to-sale conversion rises from 20% to 30%, the same budget brings 15 sales.

How to find the leaks

Where you lose peopleTypical causeWhat to do
Reach to interestA weak creative or the wrong audienceNew hooks, A/B tests
Interest to leadThe profile doesn't build trust, a vague offerA profile that sells, a clear offer
Lead to saleSlow replies, no scriptResponse time, sales script
Sale to repeatNo contact with the customerRepeat sales and LTV

How to track the funnel

A simple spreadsheet is enough for a small business: reach, visits, leads and sales every week. When lead volume grows, move to a CRM. For calculating cost per lead, see our CPL guide.

A weekly funnel check: 15 minutes

Every Monday, fill in one table: last week's reach, clicks, leads, qualified leads and sales. Then calculate each stage's conversion (next stage ÷ previous one) and compare it with the week before.

The stage where the percentage fell most is that week's main job. For example, if the number of leads hasn't changed but conversion to sales has dropped, the problem isn't the ads — it's lead handling or the offer.

One quick fix per stage

StageOne quick step
ReachBroaden the audience or add a lookalike audience
InterestChange the first 3 seconds of the creative
LeadMake the offer clearer: price, timing, what the customer gets
SaleReply to every lead within 15 minutes
Repeat purchaseGet back in touch a week after the purchase

Change only one stage at a time — that way you know exactly what moved the result.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring only the top of the funnel. Lots of reach and likes, no data on sales.
  • Blaming everything on the ads. The budget goes up while the leak stays at the sales stage.
  • Changing everything at once. Afterwards you can't tell what worked.
  • Not counting poor-quality leads. 100 leads with only 5 real ones is a completely different funnel.

Questions and answers

Is a funnel only for big companies?

No. For a small business it matters even more: the budget is small and every lost lead hurts. A simple spreadsheet and 15 minutes a week are enough to start.

Which stage should you fix first?

The one with the lowest conversion. Most often it's not the ads but the step from lead to sale: replies come late or the manager has no script.

Is the funnel the same in every industry?

The stages are similar, but the numbers differ. For expensive purchases (furniture, property) decisions take longer and weeks can pass between lead and sale. So compare your numbers with your own previous months, not with someone else's business.

Conclusion

A funnel lets you see advertising and sales as one chain. Measure each stage, find where you lose the most and fix that first — it's often cheaper than increasing the ad budget.
