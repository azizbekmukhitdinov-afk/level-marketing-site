For an ad video, the first three seconds decide everything: does the viewer stop or scroll on? But "which videos work?" is better answered by observation than by general advice. We reviewed about 60 ad Reels from marketing agencies and experts in Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, Russia and the US, and recorded the reactions to each — likes, comments, shares and saves. This article shares what we found: which approaches work, and which get almost no attention.

An important caveat: reactions aren't sales

Likes on an ad video are largely a product of paid reach. And most comments come from "write a word in the comments" mechanics rather than real discussion. So reactions show which videos hold attention, but the final verdict is leads and sales. Always compare your own videos by sales — A/B testing ad creatives.

What worked: 9 takeaways

1. The first line is a niche or a number

The best videos say who they're talking to in the very first line: "If you're a doctor…", "Owners of private clinics in Tashkent…". Or they open with a concrete number: "from 40 to 300". A generic "Want to grow your business?" barely works — Reels hooks.

2. Provocation about trust

One of the most-saved and most-shared videos played on clients' fear of "choosing the wrong contractor": "if you see this on an agency's website — run". Videos like this get forwarded to partners and saved, because people treat them as a useful vetting checklist. But the approach works without attacking competitors too — choosing an agency: red flags.

3. Humour and story

A short scene or sketch gets watched to the end as entertainment, not advertising. Two business owners talking, an everyday situation — viewers recognise themselves.

4. An honest, unpolished story

Videos where a business shows its journey without polish were saved many times: how it found its position, which formats it tried, and which flopped and got binned. Honesty about failures builds trust.

5. One concrete offer with a price

Instead of "we do everything" — one package, its price and its result. Videos like this drew more response than a general list of services — entry offer.

6. A case "in progress" with numbers

The result isn't final yet, but there are numbers: "N videos a month, this many views". People save these as instructions.

7. A useful collection or lead magnet

"We collected the 100 best ads" — ready-made material people want to get. A good way to collect contacts from interested people — lead magnet.

8. A list of professions

"Psychologist, lawyer, attorney, doctor…" — every viewer stops on hearing their own profession. Simple but effective.

9. A narrow pain everyone knows

"Leads don't pick up the phone" — a very narrow topic, but every business owner has been there. A concrete pain with concrete advice beats general topics — leads don't answer the phone.

What didn't work

FormatWhy it didn't work
Lists of logos and services ("full-service agency")No story or result — viewers don't see themselves
Interview-style reviews asking "would you recommend us?"No numbers or "before → after" — not convincing
Slides and whiteboard diagrams with no person on screenNo face or voice — attention isn't held
"Guaranteed results", "a brand is your business's image"Abstract promises that don't build trust

These formats got dozens of likes, sometimes single digits, while the best videos got thousands.

What people care about: their pains

The topics of these videos and the reactions to them make business owners' main pains clear:

  • money goes into ads but sales don't grow, or swing month to month — ads running, no sales;
  • leads are cheap but "empty" — the cheap leads trap;
  • fear of choosing the wrong agency and losing money;
  • doctors, psychologists and lawyers want clients through a personal brand but are camera-shy — Reels for experts;
  • education centres: the season is over and enrolment is short;
  • it's hard to find and keep a strong social media specialist;
  • businesses open with no marketing budget and then sit without customers — marketing before you open.

The best ad topic is one of these pains, said in the language of your niche.

A checklist for your own video

  • [ ] Is there a niche, a number or a concrete pain in the first line?
  • [ ] Is there a person on screen — a face and a voice?
  • [ ] One idea per video?
  • [ ] Is there proof: a number, a "before → after", an honest story?
  • [ ] Is the offer clear: what, for whom, how much?
  • [ ] Is there one clear action at the end — a form or DMs?
  • [ ] Does it make sense without sound — subtitles and on-screen text?

What we don't take for ourselves

The videos we reviewed also used tactics that work but that we don't use: criticising other specialists, "guarantee" promises, and logo lists without results. They can grab attention in the short term but erode trust in the long run.

Questions and answers

Can I copy competitors' videos?

Copying the script or idea — no. Studying the techniques (hook type, structure, format) and applying them with your own story — yes.

How many videos should I test?

Start with 3–5 different approaches at once, then scale up whichever wins on sales.

Do I need professional filming?

Not necessarily. In our review, the best results often came from simple, authentic videos: a person on screen with one clear idea.

Do these takeaways apply to every niche?

The core principles — niche in the first line, a person on screen, proof, one offer — are universal. The specific hook and format are tested for each niche.

Conclusion

Reviewing about 60 ad videos confirms a simple rule: a niche or number in the first line, a person on screen, an honest story and a concrete offer work; logo lists, reviews without numbers, slides without people and guarantee promises don't.
