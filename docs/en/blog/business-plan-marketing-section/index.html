Business plan: writing the marketing section, with an example

"How to write a business plan" and "business plan template" are among business owners' most frequent searches. The weakest part of a business plan is often the marketing section: one line saying "we'll advertise on Instagram and Telegram" and an unsupported "100 customers a month". That's exactly where a bank, investor or partner starts to doubt. Here's how to write the marketing section, with an example.

What the marketing section must answer

Three questions:

  1. Who will buy, and why? — audience and offer.
  2. How will they find you? — channels and budget.
  3. How much will you sell, and at what cost? — forecast and calculation.

If the section answers these with numbers and logic, it's convincing.

A sample structure: 7 parts

1. Market and audience

  • who your customer is: age, location, need, how they decide;
  • how many potential customers there are and where that number comes from;
  • signs of demand: search queries, number of competitors, surveys.

Defining your audience — target audience and customer persona. Testing demand — validate a business idea.

2. Competitors

Three to five main competitors: offer, price range, strengths and weaknesses — from public information. The aim isn't to run them down but to show your place. Method — competitor analysis.

3. Offer and positioning

In one sentence: what, for whom, what's different. This is the heart of the section — unique selling proposition.

4. Price

How the price was set: cost plus margin, competitors' range, value to the customer. Include instalments or packages if you offer them.

5. Channels

Which channels and why:

6. Budget and forecast

Banks and investors read this part most closely. Build the forecast through the funnel:

The numbers are illustrative. What matters is the basis for each assumption: where the cost per lead comes from (a test, niche experience) and where the conversion rate comes from. Give optimistic, realistic and pessimistic versions. The maths — unit economics and customer acquisition cost.

7. KPIs and control

Which metrics are tracked monthly, and what happens if the plan isn't met:

  • cost per lead, lead-to-sale rate, cost per sale;
  • repeat purchases;
  • ROMI — how to calculate ROMI.

What banks and investors look for

  • Logical numbers: not "100 customers a month" but a funnel calculation.
  • Justified assumptions: test results, a similar business's experience, a source.
  • Stated risks: "if leads cost twice as much — what will we do?"
  • Who's responsible: who runs marketing — you, an employee, an agency.

A small test before the plan

The strongest business plan includes test results. Run ads for 2–4 weeks on a small budget: real cost per lead and conversion turn the forecast from a guess into fact. A pre-opening plan — marketing before opening.

AI help

AI quickly prepares a business plan structure and draft copy, but tends to invent market figures. Check every number against a source. More in presentations with AI and what not to delegate to AI.

Common mistakes

  • A one-paragraph marketing section.
  • Unsupported numbers: "we'll capture 1% of the market".
  • A budget but no forecast — or the reverse.
  • Every channel at once.
  • No risks and no "plan B".

Questions and answers

How long should the marketing section be?

Usually 2–5 pages: tables and short explanations. Logic matters more than length.

Where do I get a cost per lead for the forecast?

Ideally from your own test. Otherwise, from a similar business's experience or a specialist's estimate, labelled as illustrative.

Where can I find a business plan template?

There are plenty of generic templates. But fill the marketing section with your own numbers — generic text convinces nobody.

Can an agency help write the marketing section?

An agency can help estimate channels, budget and forecast based on experience.

Conclusion

A business plan's marketing section answers who, why, how and how much — with numbers: audience, competitors, offer, price, channels, budget and a funnel-based forecast, plus KPIs. Justify your assumptions and test them on a small scale if you can.
