Customer testimonial video: 12 questions to ask on camera

Nobody believes "our service is excellent". But people do believe "their service helped me like this" — especially when a real person says it with their own face and voice. A customer testimonial video is one of the strongest forms of trust. Yet in many businesses these videos turn into a dull 10-second "everything was great, thanks!". Here's how to film a testimonial that actually works.

Testimonial, UGC and written review: the difference

FormatWho creates itStrength
Written reviewThe customer, on Google or InstagramBuilds trust in volume, but little emotion
UGCThe customer, on their own phoneNatural, but uncontrolled — more in UGC content
Testimonial videoYou film, the customer speaksA structured story, quality, easy to use in sales

The advantage of a testimonial is that you control the story structure: problem, decision, result. So it can answer objections precisely.

Who to choose

Your happiest customer isn't always the best hero. When choosing:

  • Similar to your ideal customer. If your main customers are young families, feature a young family. Viewers must see themselves in the hero.
  • A concrete result. Not "it was good" but "my teeth were straightened in 3 months" or "the kitchen was done in 2 weeks" (illustrative examples).
  • They had doubts. The strongest testimonial comes from someone who says "I didn't believe it at first, but...". They answer the viewer's objection.
  • Willing to talk. Someone pressured into it looks pressured on camera.

Consent comes first

The Law "On Advertising" prohibits using an individual's name or image in advertising without their consent (Article 16, lex.uz). So get written consent before filming:

  • where the video will be used: page, ads, website;
  • for how long;
  • whether to show their name;
  • what happens if the customer later withdraws consent.

Medicine and some services have extra restrictions: for example, medicine ads may not refer to thanks from people who were treated. More in medicine and supplement advertising.

Story structure: before — decision — after

A good testimonial has three parts:

  1. Before: what the problem was, what bothered them, what they feared.
  2. Decision: why they chose you, what doubts they had, how the process went.
  3. After: the result, what changed, what they'd tell others.

If you can show the "after", show it: the new kitchen, the repaired car, the student's certificate.

12 questions

Give the customer the questions in advance, but don't have them memorise answers. Keep questions open, not yes/no.

Before:

  1. What was the situation before you came to us?
  2. What bothered you most?
  3. How had you tried to solve it before?

Decision:

  1. How did you find us?
  2. What doubts did you have before choosing?
  3. What finally made you decide?
  4. What surprised you during the process?

After:

  1. What was the result — with a concrete example?
  2. What has changed now?
  3. How did reality differ from your expectations?
  4. What would you tell a friend in the same situation?
  5. How would you describe us in one sentence?

Questions 5 and 10 are the most valuable: they cover doubts and surprises — exactly what answers viewers' objections. Working with objections — content from customer objections.

Filming: the technical minimum

  • Location: the customer's home or office, or your premises — a natural setting. Clean background, no noise.
  • Sound: a lavalier mic is a must. Bad audio ruins even the best story. More in sound in video ads.
  • Framing: the customer looks at the interviewer beside the camera, not into the lens — it feels more natural.
  • B-roll: the result, the process, the customer with your staff — to show what's being said in the edit.
  • Time: a 20–30 minute conversation yields a 1–2 minute main video and 3–5 short clips.

Using testimonials in sales

Testimonials aren't just for the page. In a salesperson's hands they're a powerful tool: when a customer says "I'll think about it", the salesperson sends a video — "here's how a customer in a similar situation found it". More in "I'll think about it" follow-up.

Common mistakes

  • Writing a script for the customer — a memorised testimonial sounds fake.
  • Only praise, no problem or doubt — viewers don't believe it.
  • Bad audio.
  • No consent.
  • The result isn't shown, only talked about.
  • One long video, never cut into short clips.

Questions and answers

What if the customer doesn't want to be on camera?

Don't push. Alternatives: an audio testimonial (voice plus shots of the result) or a faceless video — hands, the result and the customer's voice.

Can I give the customer a gift for the testimonial?

A small thank-you gift is normal. But not on condition of "only if you say good things" — that makes the testimonial fake.

How many testimonial videos do I need?

A core set of 3–5, each covering a different customer type or objection. Then add a new one every quarter.

Do testimonials work well in ads?

Often, yes, especially with warm audiences in retargeting. But always test. Retargeting — how retargeting works.

Conclusion

A customer testimonial is the strongest form of trust when it's a real story: problem, doubt, decision and result. Choose the right customer, get consent, ask open questions and turn one conversation into several pieces of content.
