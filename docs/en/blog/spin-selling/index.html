SPIN selling: closing big deals with 4 types of questions

You can't push an expensive or complex product on someone in a single call. Customers need to recognise the problem — and its cost — themselves. SPIN selling is a way to get there through questions. Neil Rackham developed the method and set it out in SPIN Selling (1988), based on research into more than 35,000 sales calls.

SPIN — 4 types of question

  1. S — Situation: facts about the current state of things.
  2. P — Problem: difficulties and dissatisfaction.
  3. I — Implication: what the problem leads to and what it costs.
  4. N — Need-payoff: what the customer gains if the problem is solved.

The core idea: when customers put the value of a solution into their own words, it carries more weight than anything the rep says.

Example: B2B — selling a CRM (illustrative example)

  • Situation: "Where do you record enquiries at the moment? How many sales reps do you have?"
  • Problem: "Have enquiries ever been lost? Is it hard to see who called whom and when?"
  • Implication: "How many enquiries a month go unanswered? What might each one have been worth on average?"
  • Need-payoff: "If every enquiry got a reply within 30 minutes, what would that mean for you?"

The customer does the maths: "If we lose 20 enquiries a month, that's a lot of money." Now the CRM's price looks different — CRM for small business.

Example: services — a renovation company (illustrative example)

  • Situation: "Is the flat in a new building or an older one?"
  • Problem: "What were you unhappy with in your last renovation?"
  • Implication: "If the work overruns, how much longer would you need to rent elsewhere?"
  • Need-payoff: "How important is it to you that deadlines and penalties are written into the contract?"

When SPIN fits

  • Good fit: B2B, expensive services, long decision cycles, several decision-makers — B2B marketing.
  • Poor fit: cheap, quick purchases (coffee, clothing), where speed and convenience matter more.

Common mistakes

  • Only situation questions — the customer feels interrogated. Keep them short and do your homework beforehand.
  • Exaggerating implications — no scare tactics, just the customer's real numbers.
  • Reading a script mechanically — questions should adapt to the conversation.
  • Stopping before need-payoff questions — the customer understands the problem but doesn't see the solution.

Rolling SPIN out to your team

  • Build a bank of 5–7 questions for each stage — sales scripts.
  • Listen to calls and note which questions worked — AI call analysis.
  • Run role-plays every week.

Questions and answers

Isn't SPIN outdated?

The method dates from the 1980s, but its foundation — listening to customers and understanding their problems deeply — still works today.

Do all 4 stages have to happen in one conversation?

No. In complex deals they can be spread across several meetings.

Does SPIN work over the phone?

Yes, especially on a second call or at a meeting. On a short first call, ask just 1–2 questions.
