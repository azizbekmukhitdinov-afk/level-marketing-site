In many businesses a buyer persona is written once for a presentation and then left in a folder: "25–45, female, average income". A persona like that helps neither your ads nor your sales team. This article gives you a working persona template, shows where to get the data, walks through two filled-in examples and explains how to use the persona every day.

Buyer persona vs target audience: what's the difference

A target audience is a group: "families in Tashkent who are renovating". A buyer persona describes one typical person from that group: they have a name and a problem, and you know how they decide and what worries them. How to define an audience and split it into segments is covered in our article on target audiences. This article is the next step: building the persona from real data and turning it into a working document.

One question tells the two apart. If you handed the persona to a salesperson, could they predict the customer's first objection? If the persona only lists age and gender, no. That isn't a persona; it's demographics.

The buyer persona template: 12 fields

Keep the template to one page. Next to each field, note the source, so guesses and facts don't get mixed up.

Field 11 is the most valuable. Customers don't say "quality furniture"; they say "a sofa that survives the kids jumping on it". Your ad copy should be built from words like these.

Where to get the data

Inventing a persona at your desk is the most common mistake. In a small business the data already exists; you only need to collect it.

Call recordings

Listen to 15–20 calls from the last month: 10 that ended in a sale and the rest that didn't. From each, note three things: the first sentence (the problem), the first objection and what helped them decide. If calls aren't recorded, ask your salesperson to write down those three lines after every conversation for a week.

Your CRM

Pull three things from the CRM: how many days pass from enquiry to payment, which channels customers come from and who buys again most often. If you don't have a CRM yet, see which CRM suits a small business. Most importantly, make the "reason for loss" field mandatory. After three months it becomes your best source for the persona.

Interviews

Hold 15-minute conversations with 5–7 recent customers. Most agree in exchange for a small gift or discount. Five questions are enough:

  1. What happened before you started looking for us?
  2. Who else did you consider, and why not them?
  3. What worried you before buying?
  4. What helped you decide?
  5. How would you describe us to a friend?

Start with "what happened", not "why". People polish their reasons, but they tell their stories honestly.

People who didn't buy

Lost leads give you half the persona. Send them a short message: "What stopped you?" Few will reply, but every answer is valuable, because it shows your weakest spot.

Example 1: a furniture store in Tashkent

Illustrative example: the name and details are invented to explain the method.

  • Name: Dilnoza, 34, two children, just moved into a new flat in Yunusabad.
  • Situation: the renovation is done, the living room is empty, guests are coming.
  • Problem: the old sofa doesn't fit the new interior; she needs something sturdy for the kids.
  • Trigger: relatives are visiting for the holidays, so there's a fixed deadline.
  • Desired outcome: a good-looking sofa with washable fabric, delivered within two weeks.
  • Decision: Dilnoza chooses, her husband approves the price.
  • How she searches: watches Reels on Instagram, then finds the showroom on Yandex Maps.
  • Alternatives: bazaar shops, marketplaces.
  • Objections: "What if it doesn't look like the photo?", "What if delivery is late?"
  • Trust signals: sitting on it in the showroom, fabric samples, photos from real homes.
  • Her words: "something the kids can't ruin", "a colour that won't fade".
  • Buying process: thinks for 1–2 weeks, visits the showroom with her husband.

What goes from this persona into an ad? In a Reel, a child spills juice on the sofa and the stain wipes straight off. The offer: "delivered before the holidays". The lead form asks "When do you need it?"

Example 2: office water delivery (B2B)

Illustrative example: the company and details are made up for the explanation.

  • Name: Javohir, 29, office manager at a 40-person IT company.
  • Situation: the current supplier was late twice, and staff complained.
  • Problem: orders and invoices take up too much time.
  • Trigger: the CEO said "sort out the water issue".
  • Desired outcome: water arrives on schedule, with an electronic invoice every month.
  • Decision: Javohir prepares a proposal, the accountant checks the contract, the CEO signs.
  • How he searches: Googles "office water delivery", asks other office managers.
  • Alternatives: the current supplier, competitors offering cooler rental.
  • Objections: "What if you're late too?", "Is it hard to cancel the contract?"
  • Trust signals: a delivery-time guarantee, a trial month, a list of other offices served.
  • His words: "I don't want the headache", "the paperwork has to be right".
  • Buying process: 1–3 weeks, three people sign off.

In a B2B persona the "who decides" field matters most. You need three messages for three people, not one: convenience for the office manager, correct documents for the accountant, price and reliability for the CEO.

Using the persona in ads

In Meta ads today, broad targeting often performs well, so the persona isn't there to pick interests. It's there for the creative and the offer. The algorithm finds people based on the image and the copy, so whoever the copy speaks to is who the ad will be shown to more often.

Four things move from the persona into the ad:

  • the hook: words from field 11, the customer's own language;
  • the offer: the trigger from field 4, such as a deadline or situation;
  • the objection answer: field 9, inside the creative or in the lead form text;
  • the proof: trust signals from field 10.

One more useful technique: build a lookalike audience from the list of real buyers in your CRM. Here the persona helps you pick the right list: upload the buyers who match the persona rather than every enquiry. Details are in our article on lookalike audiences.

Make at least two creatives per persona and compare results. If the video for "Dilnoza" brings cheaper enquiries than the one for "Javohir", you've learned which persona is your core customer.

Using the persona in content and sales

In content. The objections in field 9 are ready-made post topics. "What if it doesn't look like the photo?" becomes a real video from the showroom. "What if delivery is late?" becomes a Reel showing how orders are packed in the warehouse. The method is laid out step by step in our article on content from objections.

In sales. Read the persona with your sales team and write one answer to each objection. For a new salesperson, it's the fastest training material there is.

In the customer journey. Fields 7 and 12 show where people come from and how long they deliberate. With them it's easy to build a customer journey map and see which message each stage needs.

Common mistakes

  • The persona is invented. Nobody talked to a single customer; it's just the owner's idea.
  • Too many personas. A small business needs 2–3. Ten personas means none.
  • Demographics only. Age and income are there; problems and objections aren't.
  • The document sits in a folder. Neither the sales team nor the media buyer has seen it.
  • It never gets updated. Prices, product or market have changed, but the persona hasn't.

Questions and answers

How many buyer personas do I need?

Two or three for most small businesses. The first is the customer who brings in the most money. The second is a group that's growing fast or that you want to win. If you can't make separate creatives and offers for each persona, having many of them won't help.

How many customers do I need to talk to?

To start, 5–7 interviews and 15–20 call recordings are enough. When the answers begin to repeat, the core of the persona is ready. After that, add to it every month with new calls.

How do I test a persona?

Test it in ads. If a creative built on the persona brings cheaper or better enquiries than your usual creative, the persona works. Ask your salespeople too: do the objections in the persona actually come up in conversations?

When should I update the persona?

At least every six months, and whenever price, product or your main channel changes. If cost per enquiry starts rising for no obvious reason, review the persona first; your customer may have changed.

Conclusion

A buyer persona isn't demographics; it's the story of one typical person: what happened, what worries them, the words they use and what helps them decide. Build it from call recordings, your CRM, interviews and lost deals, fit it on one page with 12 fields and use it in ads, content and sales.
