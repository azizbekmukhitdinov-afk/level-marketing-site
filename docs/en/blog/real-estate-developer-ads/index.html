Real estate developer ads: 7 mistakes and how to fix them

Running paid social ads for a real estate developer is very different from advertising a regular shop: an apartment is a family's biggest purchase, the decision takes months, and the buyer has to trust the construction company. That is why the usual "nice render + discount" approach often just burns the budget. Below are the 7 mistakes developers make most often and how to fix each one.

Why the market is competitive right now

The housing market is busy. According to the Center for Economic Research and Reforms (CERR), more than 171,000 real estate sale transactions were registered in Uzbekistan in January–June 2026, 25.3% more than a year earlier, and growth in Tashkent reached 34.9% (anhor.uz). There are plenty of buyers, but also plenty of new residential complexes. An Instagram feed shows dozens of developer ads a day, and the winner is whoever is more specific and replies to leads faster.

7 mistakes in real estate developer ads

1. Advertising with renders and "discounts" only

Everyone has 3D renders, so they no longer stand out. The buyer's main fear is "will the building be finished on time?", and a render does not answer that.

Fix: show the construction. Every month, post a short video from the site (floors, walls, utilities), an interview with the chief engineer or project manager, previously completed buildings and clear information about permits. An honest phone-shot video often builds more trust than a studio render. We covered how to shoot one in shooting Reels on a phone.

2. One ad for everyone

The same complex is bought by different people for different reasons. A young family is looking for its first home, an investor wants a studio to rent out, and a large family is thinking about 3–4 rooms and a nearby school. One generic ad does not fully land with any of them.

Fix: prepare separate creatives and offers for 3–4 segments:

SegmentWhat mattersWhat to show in the creative
First home (young family)Down payment, monthly payment, mortgagePayment calculation, small apartment layout
Large familyNumber of rooms, school, kindergarten, courtyardCourtyard, playground, nearby infrastructure
InvestorPrice, rental demand, completion dateStudio layout, location, construction stage
Movers from the regionsTrust, remote viewingVideo tour, online consultation

You don't need to narrow the audience much: Meta's algorithm finds the right people based on the creative. The segment is defined mainly by the creative and copy.

3. Collecting any lead at all

The shortest instant form gives the cheapest leads, but many of them turn out to be "just wanted to know the price". The sales team gets tired of dozens of calls a day and decides the ads are bad.

Fix: add 2–3 qualifying questions to the form: how many rooms, when they plan to buy, and the payment method (full, installments, mortgage). Leads get a bit more expensive, but salespeople talk to real buyers. To choose between a form and Direct, read lead form or messages.

4. Replying to leads late

Someone looking for an apartment leaves requests with several developers at once. Whoever calls first invites them to the office. If a lead arrives on Saturday evening and you reply on Monday, the client is already sitting in a competitor's office.

Fix: leads go straight to a CRM or a Telegram group, a manager is on duty at weekends, and the first call happens within minutes. Why this matters so much is explained in lead response time.

5. Judging ads by cost per lead alone

A cheap lead does not mean a good ad. For a developer, the key metrics are cost per sales office visit and cost per deal.

Fix: track every lead through CRM stages: lead → contacted → visited the office or site → reservation → contract. Then calculate for each campaign:

Illustrative example. A month of ads cost $2,000 and brought 200 leads, so $10 per lead. 20 of them visited the office ($100 per visit) and 2 signed a contract ($1,000 per deal). If another campaign brings leads at $25 but produces more deals, it is actually the better one.

Send this data back to Meta: if you pass "office visit" or "qualified lead" events through the Conversions API, the algorithm looks for people similar to real buyers rather than to form-fillers. For choosing a CRM, see what a CRM is.

6. Forgetting retargeting

Choosing an apartment takes several months. Someone who saw an ad once and visited the website won't decide immediately, but there is no reason to lose them.

Fix: create a separate retargeting campaign for people who watched videos, visited the site, or left a lead but didn't buy. Show them something new: construction updates, a show-flat video tour, payment terms, an open day. Build a lookalike audience from buyers who signed a contract.

7. Overpromising

Lines like "the price will double in six months" or "100% safe investment" are risky under advertising law and Meta's policies, and they also undermine trust.

Fix: write only verifiable facts: completion date, construction stage, payment terms and permits. For example, mandatory escrow accounts for off-plan construction are still at the draft-law stage, and the Ministry of Justice has said the requirement is not yet in force (spot.uz). If your project doesn't actually use escrow, don't say "escrow-protected" in your ads. If you advertise installments, state the final price and the terms clearly.

One more point: if you target the diaspora, for example compatriots in the US, Meta treats housing ads there as a special ad category and limits targeting. If Ads Manager warns you about this, don't ignore it, or the ad may be rejected.

In the first month, don't split the budget evenly across stages: put most of it into lead collection and less into awareness and retargeting, then reallocate based on the numbers. For budget planning, see how much paid advertising costs.

Pre-launch checklist

  • At least 2 creatives per segment: a video and a carousel.
  • A construction video from the past month.
  • 2–3 qualifying questions in the form.
  • Leads flow into the CRM automatically, with a responsible manager assigned.
  • Someone answers leads at weekends too.
  • CRM stages: lead → contacted → visit → reservation → contract.
  • Retargeting audiences are set up.
  • Every promise in the ads is backed by a document.

Questions and answers

What monthly budget does a developer need?

It depends on the size of the complex, prices and the sales period. First work out how much you are willing to spend to sell one apartment, then test for 1–2 months to find your real cost per visit and per deal. There is no universal figure.

Is Instagram enough, or do I need Google too?

Instagram creates demand: people see the complex even before they start searching. Google and Maps capture those already searching for "new apartments in Tashkent". Most developers do best with both; more in Google Ads vs Meta Ads.

Should I show the price in ads?

Usually yes: a concrete "from … per m²" or "payments from … a month" filters out random leads. Just make sure the number is real and the terms are clear.

When will I see results?

Leads come in from the first days, but deals usually take weeks or months. So don't switch a campaign off after a week or two because "it doesn't work": watch visits and reservations.

Conclusion

Real estate developer ads come down to trust, segments and a fast sales team. Show construction instead of renders, speak to each segment separately, qualify leads and judge ads by cost per deal.
