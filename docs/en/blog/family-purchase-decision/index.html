Family purchase decisions: who to advertise to and how to sell

"I'll check with my spouse", "I'll show my father", "We'll discuss it as a family and get back to you" — salespeople at furniture stores, developers, car dealerships, education centres and clinics hear this every day. In Uzbekistan, many big purchases are made not by one person but by a family. Yet ads and sales usually target only the person who submitted the lead. They say "yes", and at home the answer is "no". Here's how to build ads and sales around family decisions.

Who takes part in the decision

A big purchase usually involves several roles. One person may hold several, or they may be spread across different people:

RoleWhat they doExample (illustrative: a kitchen)
InitiatorFeels the need, starts searchingWife: "We need a new kitchen"
UserUses the productThe whole family, mainly the wife
AdviserGives opinions, influencesMother-in-law, a craftsman they know
PayerProvides the moneyHusband or the family budget
DeciderGives the final "yes"Often husband and wife together

The person who submits the lead is usually the initiator. But the decision lies with someone else. If your sales process convinces only the initiator, they have to retell your arguments at home in their own words — and that often fails.

Where this matters most

  • Real estate — a family's biggest purchase.
  • Cars.
  • Furniture and renovation — the home belongs to everyone.
  • Children's education — kindergarten, school, tutoring: the child uses it, the parents decide. More in marketing a private kindergarten or school.
  • Healthcare — especially expensive treatment and care for older relatives.
  • Wedding services — many family members are involved. More in marketing a wedding venue.

Advertising: who to show ads to

Audience

Meta has no "family decision" interest. But there are several approaches:

  • a broad audience: the algorithm finds the interested person — the initiator;
  • a separate creative for the second decision-maker: for example, one version of a kitchen ad for the wife (design, convenience), another for the husband (price, durability, warranty);
  • retargeting: for people who visited the site or watched the video — proof, guarantees, instalments.

Working with audiences — target audience and customer persona.

Creative: arguments for both sides

One creative can speak to both roles: an emotional part (beautiful and convenient) and a rational part (price, warranty, timing). For example: "A new kitchen in 2 weeks. 5-year warranty. From 900,000 soum a month" (illustrative example).

Sales: working with the second person

1. Find out early who decides

In the first conversation, ask politely:

Who else will be making the decision with you? Shall I prepare information for them too?

It isn't rude — it saves the customer time.

2. A "take-home" pack for the second person

At home, the customer speaks on your behalf. Help them:

  • a one-page summary: what, price, timing, warranty;
  • a 1–2 minute video: product, process, a customer review — customer testimonial video;
  • answers to the most common questions.

All in a format easy to forward on Telegram.

3. Invite both to a meeting

The strongest move is inviting the decision-makers together: to the showroom, the office or a measurement visit.

If you come together with your spouse on Saturday, I'll show you both everything.

When both see it at the same time and ask their questions, the decision comes faster and "yes" is more likely.

4. Follow up on a family timeline

Family decisions aren't quick. Your follow-up plan should allow for that: "Talk it over with your family — shall I message you on Thursday?" More in "I'll think about it" follow-up.

Objections: who worries about what

Different roles have different objections:

WhoMain concernAnswer
InitiatorWill it look good and be convenientPortfolio, 3D design, samples
PayerPrice, extra costsPrice breakdown, instalments, warranty
AdviserQuality, "will they cheat us"Reviews, process, certificates

Prepare content for each concern — more in content from customer objections.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the person who submitted the lead as the only decision-maker.
  • Answering "I'll consult my family" with "fine, I'll wait" and giving them nothing.
  • Materials that don't answer the second person's questions.
  • Inviting only one person to the meeting.
  • Ignoring how long family decisions take and marking the customer "lost" after 1–2 days.

Questions and answers

Do I need separate creatives for men and women?

Not always, but for expensive family purchases it's worth testing. Compare by leads and sales.

How do I invite the second decision-maker to a meeting?

Think about their convenience: a weekend, an evening, an online video call. And give a reason: "I'll answer both your questions at once."

Does this work in B2B?

Yes — instead of a family, there's procurement, a director and an accountant. The logic is the same. More in B2B marketing.

How long does a family decision take?

It depends on the price: days to weeks for furniture, weeks to months for an apartment. Adjust your follow-up plan accordingly.

Conclusion

In a big purchase, the person who submits the lead is often just the initiator, and the family makes the decision. Identify the decision-makers early, give the second person a take-home pack, invite both to a meeting and match your follow-up to the family's timeline. In real estate, furniture and education, Level Marketing looks at ads and sales together — because the person who submits the lead and the person who says "yes" are often different people.
